## Abstract 

Causal representation learning aims to discover robust features by exploiting the causal structure underlying data generation. Existing methods require specifying the causal structure a priori, yet different structures demand fundamentally incom-patible invariance constraints, and misspecification leads to representations that discard predictive information. We introduce SaCRL, a framework that jointly identifies the causal structure and learns the corresponding invariant representation without prior structural knowledge. Our approach formulates structure selection as a soft optimization over candidate invariances using HSIC-based violation metrics, with adaptive weights that automatically concentrate on the achievable structure. We provide theoretical guarantees for structure identification, including under random-feature approximation, invariance satisfaction, and out-of-distribution generalization. Empirically, SaCRL recovers the true structure on synthetic and semi-synthetic Bayesian-network benchmarks, outperforms fixed-invariance base-lines on Colored MNIST, achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on three DomainBed benchmarks (PACS, VLCS, OfficeHome), and degrades gracefully under struc-tural misspecification and limited environment diversity. Code is available at: 

https://github.com/ArmanBehnam/sacrl .

## 1 Introduction 

Causal representation learning [55] has emerged as a principled approach to building robust models by uncovering the underlying mechanisms that generate data, enabling generalization beyond training distributions [55, 45]. Current approaches are broadly categorized into two paradigms, each with distinct structural assumptions. Structure-based methods [73, 60, 6] require explicit knowledge of Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) and complete Structural Causal Models (SCMs), imposing significant barriers when causal structures are unknown. Distribution-invariant methods [4, 30] avoid an explicit graph but enforce a fixed invariance across environments, committing to one causal structure. When that commitment is wrong, the enforced invariance discards predictive signal.

Different causal structures demand incompatible invariance properties [40, 74]. A representation optimized for one structure performs poorly under another, as shown in Figure 2. The central question we address is can we learn optimal invariant representations without knowing the causal structure? This requires a paradigm shift from structure-specific to structure-agnostic 

learning 1: jointly discover causal mechanisms generating the data and learn representations tailored to those mechanisms. The challenge is both computational (avoiding exponential structure search) and theoretical (providing guarantees when structure itself is uncertain). We introduce a kernel-based framework, Structure-agnostic Causal Representation Learning (SaCRL) ,for joint structure discovery and representation learning. SaCRL generalizes Anti-Causal Invariant Abstractions (ACIA) [7], which learns invariant representations for the anti-causal structure G1, to settings where the structure itself is unknown. The key theoretical insight is that under the true causal structure, the corresponding invariance is achievable by predictive representations, whereas wrong invariances incur irreducible violation. This asymmetry enables structure identification via a SoftMin objective that automatically concentrates on the correct structure. We prove: (i) consistent structure identification, which also holds under random-feature approximation of HSIC, (ii) bounds on the remaining invariance violation, (iii) out-of-distribution generalization bounds, and (iv) sample complexity for joint identification and generalization. Empirically, SaCRL also recovers the true structure on semi-synthetic Bayesian-network data with known ground truth, and its adaptive weights provide a calibrated confidence for the selected structure.

## 3 Problem Formulation 

3.1 Motivation 

Different causal structures demand fundamentally incompatible invariance properties. An anti-causal setting ( Y → X) requires representations conditionally independent of environments given the target. A confounded-descendant setting ( X → Y with confounding) requires the predictive distribution 

P (Y |ϕ(X)) to be invariant. A confounded-outcome setting requires the marginal P (ϕ(X)) to be invariant. A representation optimized for one structure typically performs poorly under another. Suppose the true structure is confounded-descendant ( G2), but we enforce anti-causal invariance (I1 : ϕ(X) ⊥ E|Y ). This constraint forces the representation to discard information that varies with E conditional on Y , but in G2, this includes precisely the causal signal X → Y . The learned representation becomes predictively useless. We observe that the correct invariance is achievable (the representation can satisfy it while maintaining prediction accuracy), whereas incorrect invariances are unachievable (satisfying them requires discarding predictive information).

Assumption 1 (Sufficient Environment Diversity) . The training environments exhibit variation along three axes: marginal distribution P (X), conditional P (X|Y ), and outcome P (Y |X). We assume the three causal structures are distinguishable: testing I1 requires variation in P (X|Y ), testing I2

requires variation in P (Y |X), and testing I3 requires variation in P (X).

Optimization: The problem admits a bilevel decomposition, with the structure weights α in the outer problem and (ϕ, w ) in the inner problem (Appendix F.1).The optimization problem is challenging due to (i) the non-convex loss landscape with multiple local minima, and (ii) the difficulty of jointly optimizing all variables. To address these challenges, we adopt the following observations and optimization strategies: 1. Single-loop relaxation: Instead of solving the bilevel optimization problem explicitly, we employ a single-loop algorithm in which α is implicitly determined via a SoftMin operator. 2. Multi-start initialization: We run the optimization from M ≥ 10 random initializations and select the solution with the lowest objective value L.3. Temperature annealing: We initialize the SoftMin temperature β with a small value to allow soft selection, and gradually increase it to βmax to encourage hard selection. 4. Validation-based selection: Among the resulting stationary points, we select the model that achieves the best performance on held-out environments. Because V1 and V2 condition on the current ϕ, the conditioning target moves during training. Our guarantees (Section 6) are stated at the converged representation, where conditioning is on a fixed ϕ.In practice, the ERM warm start, temperature annealing, and cross-fitted residuals (Appendix H.7) stabilize training, and conditioning on an exponential-moving-average (EMA) copy of the encoder gives nearly identical results (Appendix H.8). ## 6 Theoretical Guarantees 

We establish four theoretical results: (1) violation characterization showing the correct structure is identifiable, (2) finite-sample structure identification holding under random-feature approximation (Corollary 1), (3) representation optimality, and (4) out-of-distribution generalization bounds. The details of the theoretical results and proofs are discussed in Appendix D and Appendix E, respectively. 

## 7 Experiments 

We evaluate SaCRL across synthetic and real-world settings, addressing four questions: Q1: Can the method identify the true causal structure from data? Q2: Does correct structure identification improve OOD generalization? Q3: Does SaCRL outperform fixed-invariance baselines on standard domain generalization benchmarks? Q4: How does it behave under structural misspecification and limited environment diversity? Additional experiments are deferred to Appendix H .

7.1 Experimental Setup Baselines. We compare against: ERM (no invariance), IRM [4] (enforces I2, i.e., assumes G2), 

VREx [30] (variance-based regularization), and CIRCE [47] (enforces I1, i.e., assumes G1). 

Metrics. For synthetic data: we use structure identification accuracy (percentage of runs identifying the correct Gk) and OOD test accuracy on a held-out environment with larger distribution shift. For real-world datasets, we use OOD accuracy on the anti-correlated test environment. Results are means over 5 random seeds, and ± denotes the standard deviation across seeds. 

Implementation Details. For synthetic data, we use 3-layer MLPs with hidden dimensions [256, 128] for both encoder and predictor. For Colored MNIST, we use a 4-layer CNN encoder with base channels 32. For PACS, VLCS, and OfficeHome, we use a frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet18 followed by an MLP encoder with hidden dimensions [256, 128] and a linear predictor; the same model produces both the identified structures (Table 4) and the accuracies (Table 5). We select the invariance penalty weight λ ∈ { 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0} per experiment by OOD accuracy, i.e., test-domain (oracle) selection in the terminology of Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz [20] ( λ=5 .0 for Section 7.6; sensitivity in Appendix H.3), and use CVaR with ρ = 0 .5. We train with Adam optimizer (learning rate 10 −4 for synthetic, 5 × 10 −5 for images) using balanced environment sampling. Random Fourier Features (RFF) with D = 1000 dimensions are used to approximate HSIC, reducing memory from

## 8 Conclusion 

We proposed a structure-agnostic approach to causal representation learning that formulates causal structure selection as a soft optimization problem over HSIC-based invariance violation metrics. The key insight is that correct invariances can be achieved with zero violation while preserving predictive accuracy, whereas incorrect invariances cannot, enabling automatic structure identification through optimization. We developed (i) a unified measure-theoretic framework that extends prior work on anti-causal learning to confounded-descendant and confounded-outcome settings; (ii) an efficient learning algorithm whose complexity scales linearly with the number of candidate structures, leveraging SoftMin aggregation and RFF-based HSIC approximations; and (iii) theoretical guarantees for structure identification, under random-feature approximation, invariance satisfaction, and OOD generalization. Empirically, SaCRL recovers the true structure on synthetic and semi-synthetic Bayesian-network benchmarks with a calibrated confidence, surpasses fixed-invariance baselines on Colored MNIST and three DomainBed benchmarks, and remains robust under bidirectional dynamics and limited environment diversity.

18 A Related Work 

Distribution-Invariant Learning. A large body of work in domain adaptation and domain general-ization aims to learn representations that are invariant across environments or distributions. Early non-causal approaches such as (C-)ADA [37] and DDAIG [86] focus on aligning feature distributions across domains. More recent methods, including FeAT [14] and AIA [63], design more expressive objectives to improve robustness under distribution shift. Representative domain generalization methods include ERM [68], DOMAINBED [20], L2A-OT [87], StableNet [84], SagNets [42], SWAD [12], FACT [77], Ratatouille [51], XRM [46], and IRM [4], along with extensions such as REx [30], counterfactual invariance [69], information bottleneck approaches [1], causal domain adaptation [74], and transportable representations [24]. While effective in practice, these methods generally enforce distributional invariance without explicitly reasoning about the underlying causal structure or validating whether the assumed invariance is causally appropriate. Weight-averaging methods such as SWAD [12] modify the optimization trajectory rather than the objective, and are complementary to SaCRL, which determines which invariance is enforced. 

Intervention-based Causal Learning. Intervention-based causal representation learning leverages environment changes or interventions to identify causal factors. Foundational works include nonpara-metric ICR [72], which jointly learns representations and intervention targets under SCM assumptions, and general nonlinear mixing models [9], which address nonlinear latent interactions. Several weakly supervised approaches [8, 38, 39, 59] exploit partial intervention information to learn invariant representations, while ICRL [2] extends these ideas to imperfect interventions. Weak distributional invariance [3] studies identifiability under perfect and imperfect interventions by identifying latent variables with stable distributional properties. Unsupervised approaches such as ICA-based causal learning [75] aim to recover latent causal variables through component analysis and statistical independence criteria. AIT [53] assumes explicit SCMs with known DAG structures and focuses on identifying standard causal directions. These methods typically rely on either explicit intervention signals or strong assumptions about the form of the causal graph. 

Structure-based Causal Representation Learning. Structure-based causal representation learning methods explicitly assume or model a particular causal structure. DISRL [74] and Causal Disentan-glement [61] learn representations aligned with latent causal graphs, while classical approaches such as ICP [44] and its nonlinear extension [21] exploit invariance across environments to identify causal mechanisms. Related works such as CSG [34] and LECI [19] focus on identifying causal subgraphs and removing spurious correlations, often under DAG assumptions. KCDC [41] employs kernel-based independence tests for causal discovery and orientation. Anti-causal modeling approaches [40] study settings where inputs are generated from labels and protected attributes using conventional causal models and do-calculus, while [7] develops measure-theoretic formulations for anti-causal representation learning. 

Research Gap. Existing approaches face a trilemma. Structure-based methods achieve strong guarantees but require knowing the causal graph. Invariant learning methods avoid explicit graphs but hardcode implicit structural assumptions that cause failures under mismatch. Causal discovery methods learn structure but decouple it from representation learning, preventing joint optimization. No existing framework simultaneously discovers causal structure and learns representations adapted to that structure in a unified, end-to-end differentiable objective. Our work fills this gap: we introduce a structure-agnostic objective that evaluates representations against all candidate causal structures, automatically concentrating on the correct one through adaptive weighting, while jointly optimizing predictive performance.

C.3 Interventions and the Do-Calculus 

Interventions distinguish causal relationships from statistical associations. We formalize both perfect (hard) and imperfect (soft) interventions within the generalized framework. 

Definition C.8 (Perfect and Imperfect Interventions) . An intervention on a causal structure S is specified by a measurable mapping Q : F × Ω → [0 , 1] where F is a suitable σ-algebra. (i) A perfect (hard) intervention on variable V replaces its causal mechanism KV with a fixed distribution Q independent of parent values: Q(A | ω) = Q(A) for all ω ∈ Ω, A ∈ F

(ii) An imperfect (soft) intervention on variable V modifies its causal mechanism to depend on both parents and intervention parameters: Q(A | ω) varies with ω ∈ Ω

The do-operator do (X = x) corresponds to a perfect intervention that sets X to value x by replacing the kernel KX with a point mass at x, formally: Q(A) = I{x}(A) for measurable sets A.

Definition C.9 (Causal Independence) . Random variables X and Y are causally independent given 

Z, denoted X ⊥⊥c Y | Z, if 

P (Y | do (X = x), Z = z) = P (Y | Z = z) (15) for all x in the support of X and all z in the support of Z, where do (X = x) denotes an intervention setting X to value x.The do-operator do (X = x) differs fundamentally from P (Y | X = x) which observes X = x

H.6 Semi-Synthetic Bayesian-Network Benchmarks Construction. For each benchmark, we select a target Y in a published network, take X as its Markov blanket, and induce environments by soft interventions on a latent parent outside the blanket. The canonical structure is read off the published DAG by the rules of Section 2. G3 requires two independent root components reconverging at Y , which ALARM (12 roots) supplies twice and ASIA (2 roots) once; G1 is not derivable under this recipe, since it requires the Markov blanket of Y to consist only of descendants of Y . Before training, each derived structure is verified in simulation: its own invariance holds while the competing one fails. We keep a benchmark only if the competing-invariance violation exceeds 5.0 standard deviations of the permutation null. 

Departures from the published networks. The SACHS consensus DAG fits its own observational data weakly, so SACHS requires larger interventions to produce contrast. For ASIA, we keep the published structure but re-parameterize two conditional probability tables whose published marginals leave the exogenous branch nearly constant. ALARM requires no adjustment. 

Discussion. Five benchmarks span two derivable structures across three networks (three G2, two G3), so a method that returns a single structure by default is correct on at most 3/5. alarm_co separates more cleanly than asia_g2 (28.3 vs. 24.6 null standard deviations) but has lower accuracy, because its target has three classes over 12 one-hot columns rather than two. alarm_g2 barely clears the 5.0 threshold and is correspondingly the least reliable. The two G3 benchmarks come from different networks, so their agreement is not an artifact of a single graph.

## I Limitation 

Our framework targets three canonical structures arising under partial observability and confounding-mediated environment effects; cyclic systems, multi-confounder dynamics, or settings where E

directly rewires X–Y fall outside this scope , though the heterogeneous extension and the empirical robustness study (Section 7.6) show graceful degradation under misspecification. In such cases, SaCRL signals the mismatch: if min k Vk exceeds the permutation threshold τα (Definition G.2), the data do not support a canonical structure, and the selected structure should not be interpreted as identified. Structure identification further requires sufficient environment diversity (Assumption 1) and at least m ≥ 3 training environments (Appendix H.10); under near-identical environments, the invariance penalty loses informativeness even though identification remains stable. Conditional HSIC estimation depends on kernel regression quality and may benefit from cross-fitting in low-sample regimes, per results in Table 14. 

## J Applications and Broader Connections 

SaCRL addresses a question that recurs across several fields: which invariance should a model enforce when the causal structure of the data is unknown? We discuss five areas where this question arises, review recent work in each, and state what SaCRL adds. 

Identifiability beyond an assumed structure. A central open problem in representation learning is identifiability : whether the data determine the learned representation uniquely, up to simple transformations such as permutation and element-wise reparameterization, so that it recovers the factors that generated the data rather than one of many equally predictive alternatives. Without identifiability, a representation carries no guarantee of meaning, and the guarantees built on it, including robustness under distribution shift, do not follow. The foundational results are negative: nonlinear independent component analysis is not identifiable in general [22], and unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without inductive biases on both the model and the data [36]. Progress 44 since then has come from adding structure. Auxiliary variables such as environment labels [27], paired views from data augmentation [71], and interventions [2] each restore identifiability, and together they define the program of causal representation learning [55]. Each of these results, however, relies on a generative structure supplied by the analyst: which variables cause which, and where environments intervene. The structure itself is never identified; it enters as an assumption, and when the assumption is wrong the guarantees no longer apply. SaCRL moves identification one level up. Within the three canonical structures, it identifies from multi-environment data which structure generated the data, and therefore which invariance a representation should satisfy. 

Human–AI interaction and longitudinal behavioral modeling. Conversational AI systems now interact with the same people over weeks and months, which makes human–AI interaction a lon-gitudinal, two-way process: users adapt to the system, and the system adapts to users through personalization, memory, and training on their feedback. Recent evidence shows why this process must be modeled causally. In a four-week randomized study with about 1,000 participants and more than 300,000 messages, the assigned chatbot conditions had no significant effect on psychosocial out-comes, whereas participants who chose to use the chatbot more reported worse outcomes [16], which leaves open whether heavier use drives these outcomes or reflects them. Language models tailor their answers to the beliefs users express [58], so the user’s state shapes the model’s behavior; conversely, deployed predictions and recommendations change the very behavior they later learn from [43, 23]. Preferences also differ systematically across people and cultures, as PRISM documents by linking 1,500 participants from 75 countries to 8,011 live conversations with 21 language models [28]. The gap is that any model of user or assistant behavior over time must decide what to hold invariant across users, cultures, and time periods, and that decision depends on the direction of influence. If the modeled signal is generated by the target, as when a user’s latent state generates their messages, the anti-causal invariance I1 applies; if the signal drives the target, as when the assistant’s responses drive the user’s next action, the causal invariances I2 or I3 apply; and if influence runs both ways, the system is in a feedback loop that no single invariance describes. SaCRL gives this decision a statistical footing. Treating users, cultural groups, or time windows as environments, it selects the invariance supported by the data, assigns different structures to different behavioral signals through its heterogeneous extension (Appendix G), and, when influence is bidirectional, keeps its structure weights spread instead of committing to a wrong structure (Section 7.6, Appendix H.4). The feedback regime thus becomes observable rather than silently misspecified. Cyclic dynamics lie outside the three canonical structures, and extending the candidate set to dynamic, feedback-coupled structures is a natural next step toward causally grounded models of long-term human–AI relationships. 

Multi-site clinical prediction and the direction of causation. Clinical models are trained at some hospitals and deployed at others, and dataset shift between sites is a leading cause of failure. A pneumonia detector trained on chest radiographs degraded at external hospitals, in part because it exploited site-specific signals [79]; a widely deployed proprietary sepsis model performed markedly worse under external validation than its developer reported [76]; and clinicians are now urged to treat dataset shift as a routine deployment risk [17]. A causal view explains why remedies differ. In anti-causal tasks the disease generates the image, so shifts appear as changes in prevalence or manifestation, whereas in causal tasks the inputs generate the outcome [11]. Dataset biases that look identical in the data can therefore demand different mitigation strategies [25], and shift-stable modeling requires the analyst to specify the causal graph [62]. The gap is that the causal direction must be declared by hand, per task and often per variable: symptoms are anti-causal, biomarkers are not (Figure 1). SaCRL treats each hospital as an environment and infers the direction from data. Theorem D.2 bounds the probability of selecting a wrong structure by 6 exp( −nη 2/(2048 κ4)) , the heterogeneous extension assigns a structure to each variable (Appendix G), and the permutation test of Section 5 flags cases where no canonical structure fits, so a clinical team knows when the selected structure should not be trusted. 

Domain adaptation and generalization: which distribution to align. Methods for learning under distribution shift differ in which part of the joint distribution they hold fixed across domains. 

Marginal alignment makes P (ϕ(X)) invariant, as in domain-adversarial training [18]; conditional alignment makes P (ϕ(X) | Y ) invariant, as under target and conditional shift [83] and generalized label shift [65]; and covariate-shift methods such as IRM make P (Y | ϕ(X)) invariant [4]. The appropriate assumption depends on the causal direction [54]: label shift is natural in anti-causal tasks and can be corrected with black-box predictors [33], while marginal alignment provably fails when label distributions differ across domains [85]. Recent work makes this dependence explicit: 45 each causal graph entails different independence constraints, so any algorithm built on a single fixed constraint fails on some shifts [26, 74], consistent with the finding that no domain generalization method reliably outperforms ERM [20]. Adaptive methods such as CACM [26] still take the graph as input. SaCRL removes this requirement. Its three invariances are exactly these three alignment targets, I3 (marginal), I1 (conditional), and I2 (covariate shift), measured on a common HSIC scale and selected by SoftMin without the graph. Theorem D.1 shows that a predictive representation can satisfy only the invariance of the true structure, and the experiments show the cost of a wrong choice: on anti-causal Colored MNIST, IRM, which enforces I2, falls below ERM (21.3% vs. 27.2%), while SaCRL reaches 45.2% (Table 3). 

Algorithmic fairness: independence, separation, and sufficiency. The three main group-fairness criteria constrain a score or representation R with respect to a sensitive attribute A: independence 

(demographic parity) requires R ⊥⊥ A, separation (equalized odds) requires R ⊥⊥ A | Y , and 

sufficiency (calibration within groups) requires Y ⊥⊥ A | R [5]. Impossibility results show that these criteria cannot hold simultaneously outside degenerate cases [29, 15]. Recent causal analyses show that the appropriate criterion depends on the data-generating graph: in anti-causal prediction, separation coincides with a notion of risk invariance [40], and whether data balancing achieves a given criterion depends on the graph [56]. The gap is that practitioners choose a criterion without knowing which one the graph permits at no cost in accuracy. With A in the role of E, SaCRL’s invariances are precisely these criteria: I3 is independence, I1 is separation, and I2 is sufficiency, with V3, V1, and V2 measuring their violations. When A enters the system as E does in Figure 3, Theorem D.1 gives a causal counterpart of the impossibility results: a predictive representation can satisfy at most the criterion matching the true structure, that criterion is achievable when X⊥ 

> z

is predictive, and the adaptive weights reveal which one it is. Since the predictor is a function of ϕ(X),independence and separation of ϕ(X) carry over to the score. The choice of a fairness criterion remains a normative decision; SaCRL informs it by showing which criterion the data can support. 

Diagnosing and explaining distribution shift. A related line of work asks not how to be robust, but what changed. Shift detection tests whether training and deployment distributions differ [49]. 

Shift attribution explains a performance drop by the mechanisms that changed, either using a known causal graph [80] or by decomposing the drop into a change in the covariate distribution ( X-shift) and in the outcome given the covariates ( Y | X-shift) [10]. A benchmark of natural tabular shifts found Y | X-shifts to be the most prevalent and showed that conclusions about algorithms depend on the type of shift [35]. These tools explain the failure of a fixed model after deployment; they do not decide what a model should be invariant to during training. SaCRL connects the two. Its diversity matrix D (Definition C.19) measures, across training environments, the marginal shift in 

P (X), the conditional shift in P (X | Y ), and the outcome shift in P (Y | X) with kernel mean embeddings; the first and third are the X- and Y | X-shifts of the attribution literature, computed between training environments rather than between training and deployment. A permutation check on D before training determines which invariances are testable (Appendix C.6), and training then enforces the one the data support, so diagnosis and remedy become parts of a single objective. 

Invariance-based causal discovery and conditional independence testing. Heterogeneity across environments is also a source of causal knowledge. Invariant causal prediction identifies the causal parents of a target as the variables whose conditional distribution is stable across environments [44], and its nonlinear extension replaces linear models with nonparametric conditional independence tests [21]; interventional single-cell data, such as the protein-signaling measurements of Sachs et al. [52], remain standard testbeds. These methods rest on conditional independence testing, where kernel tests are widely used [82] and recent split-sample variants improve calibration [48]. A fundamental limit applies: no conditional independence test can control its level over all null distributions while retaining power against all alternatives [57], so useful guarantees require explicit assumptions. Existing methods test invariances of observed variables and return a set of parents; they neither handle high-dimensional inputs such as images nor learn a representation. SaCRL tests invariances of a learned representation ϕ(X) and learns ϕ jointly with the test. Its guarantees are stated under explicit assumptions consistent with this limit, namely an additive-noise model for conditional HSIC (Lemma 2) and a positive faithfulness gap η (Definition D.2). On semi-synthetic benchmarks built from the ASIA, ALARM, and SACHS networks, SaCRL selects the structure fixed by the published graph in 75–95% of runs (Table 2). 46
