Abstract. Continual learning (CL) in text classification faces two critical challenges: catastrophic forgetting and negative transfer across sequential tasks. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods such as LoRA enable efficient adaptation by learning low-rank updates of the model parameters. However, these compact representations are normally trained in isolation, limiting their reuse across related tasks. We introduce Task-Oriented Rank Adaptation (TORA), a geometric routing framework that leverages the low-rank structure of LoRA adapters to decide whether to transfer knowledge from the most compatible expert ( Boosting ) or isolate the new task ( Shielding ) based on structural similarity. Evaluated across 15 diverse text classification benchmarks, TORA consistently avoids harmful routing decisions: compatible tasks exceed their isolated performance while reducing training time, and structurally distant tasks are protected from interference with no loss in accuracy. With a single geometric threshold and no reliance on task identities or predefined sequences, TORA provides a simple and effective approach for dynamic adapter routing in sequential text classification systems. 

Keywords: Continual Learning · Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning · Adapter Routing · Task Geometry · Text Classification. 

## 1 Introduction 

Transformer architectures have established the fundamental baseline for Text Classification applications [20]. However, extending these architectures to operate in sequential and dynamic environments introduces the Continual Learning (CL) problem, a machine learning paradigm that involves training a model on a sequential stream of tasks [4]. The primary objective is to adapt to new domains while maintaining high performance on previously seen tasks, even when access to historical training data is no longer available. When a neural network is trained incrementally across multiple domains, it is highly susceptible to catastrophic forgetting [13], a phenomenon where learning new representations overwrites and degrades previously learned parameters. Furthermore, adapting the model to new data distributions often leads to negative transfer [25], where the interference between unrelated tasks deteriorates the overall accuracy and stability of the system. arXiv:2610.01702v1 [cs.CL] 1 Oct 2026 2 R. Sánchez et al. 

To mitigate catastrophic forgetting, recent literature has heavily adopted Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) mechanisms [6]. Specifically, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) encapsulates task-specific learning into small, low-dimensional weight matrices [8]. By keeping the original pre-trained model frozen, LoRA perfectly protects the base knowledge from being overwritten. However, this architectural separation also becomes a major limitation in continual learning scenarios, because these standard PEFT modules operate in strict isolation, the learned representations remain stored within their specific adapters. This strict separation prevents positive transfer between tasks with similar data distributions [2]. Consequently, models are forced to learn similar concepts from Scratch for every new task, ignoring the computational advantages of knowledge reuse and unnecessarily increasing the number of parameters. We introduce Task-Oriented Rank Adaptation (TORA), a dynamic routing framework that overcomes the limitations of static adapter isolation in continual learning. In TORA, each previously learned LoRA adapter is treated as an expert, and the shared SVD reference space is constructed from the collection of all learned experts. Incoming tasks are projected into this common geometric space, where structural affinity is estimated through cosine similarity [14] between the new task and the stored experts. When affinity is detected, the router transfers knowledge from the most compatible expert to accelerate convergence ( Boosting ); otherwise, it enforces parameter isolation to prevent negative interference ( Shielding ) [15]. We validate this framework through a geometric analysis of 15 text classification benchmarks and a Monte Carlo simulation, demonstrating empirical advantages over static baselines in both accuracy and parameter efficiency. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 

– We propose Task-Oriented Rank Adaptation (TORA) , a geometric routing framework that estimates task affinity directly from LoRA adaptation weights in a shared SVD reference space, eliminating the need for semantic routing or trainable gating networks. 

– We introduce a compact geometric task fingerprint that represents each LoRA adapter as a low-dimensional structural signature. These fingerprints enable efficient similarity estimation while avoiding computationally expensive manifold-based comparisons, making dynamic routing practical for continual learning. 

– We validate the proposed framework on 15 text classification benchmarks and Monte Carlo simulations, demonstrating improved routing reliability, positive transfer, and computational savings over isolated training. 

## 2 Related Work 

In text classification, CL addresses the challenge of training models on sequential data streams without suffering from catastrophic forgetting. Early measures primarily focused on regularization, which penalizes changes to essential weights [10], and rehearsal mechanisms, which store [3] or generate [19] examples of past Task-Oriented Rank Adaptation 3

domains. Although these methods prove effective, they impose limitations in memory and computational cost. Conversely, recent paradigms highlight a positive aspect of sequential learning: accumulating knowledge across multiple domains can actively enhance a model’s few-shot adaptation capabilities [9]. Building upon these insights, the proposed TORA framework transitions from expensive data-replay and complex regularization strategies to a purely parameter-efficient geometric routing. By evaluating structural task affinities, TORA dynamically transfers past knowledge to accelerate convergence on related domains, while strictly isolating incompatible parameters to prevent interference. To reduce the cost of adapting large language models, PEFT methods such as LoRA have become standard. LoRA freezes the base model and learns low-rank updates, allowing multiple tasks to be trained one after another in the same model. This makes training more efficient and scalable. However, it still does not define how knowledge should be shared or separated between tasks. Recent research has proposed dynamic architectural approaches to overcome static isolation. Methods such as orthogonal adaptation [23] and multi-task dynamic routing [24] attempt to project new knowledge into distinct subspaces or learn unified representations. While these approaches improve parameter distribution, they often rely on trainable routing mechanisms or computationally expensive similarity estimation, and still lack a simple criterion to evaluate the structural compatibility between sequential tasks before initialization. This reveals a gap for a lightweight routing strategy that directly exploits the geometry of adapter parameters. TORA addresses this gap by representing each task as a compact geometric fingerprint in a shared SVD reference space, enabling deterministic structural similarity estimation without semantic representations, trainable routing modules, or computationally expensive manifold operations. 

## 3 The TORA Framework 

TORA evaluates the geometric compatibility between sequential domains before parameter initialization, replacing the default assumption of task isolation in PEFT methods. An expert is defined as a task-specific LoRA adapter trained on a given domain and stored in a memory pool, encapsulating its optimized weights without interfering with previously learned distributions. Figure 1 illustrates the high-level operation of the framework. Specifically, the continual learning cycle works as follows: when a new task arrives, it is trained for one epoch to get a partial adapter. The SVD of its weights is then computed to extract its geometric fingerprint ( ϕnew ). This representation is compared against the reference space built from the previously stored experts. If the structural similarity is above the angular threshold, the router triggers a transfer protocol, loading the weights of the most compatible expert as a starting point for the new task. If not, the shielding protocol is activated and new adaptation matrices ( A and B) are trained from scratch. Once training is complete, the resulting adapter is saved to the memory pool, updating the reference space for any future tasks. 4 R. Sánchez et al. 

The framework builds upon the mathematical foundation of LoRA. During standard training, LoRA freezes a pre-trained weight matrix W0 ∈ Rd×d and constrains its update by representing it with a low-rank decomposition ∆W = BA .The matrices are defined as B ∈ Rd×r and A ∈ Rr×d, where the rank r ≪ d. The forward pass is thus modified to: 

W = W0 + ∆W = W0 + BA 

Within this formulation, matrix A acts as a feature extractor that maps the input down to a lower-dimensional, information-dense subspace. Matrix 

B subsequently projects these representations back to the original dimension. By capturing task-specific knowledge in these low-rank matrices, the model significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters. To enable dynamic routing, TORA evaluates the structural affinity of these learned projections. During the training phase of a new task, TORA applies SVD to the existing adapter weight matrices to isolate their core semantic directions. For a given adaptation matrix ∆W , the decomposition is defined as: 

∆W = U ΣV T

where the orthogonal matrix U contains the left singular vectors that define the geometric rotation in the output space, the diagonal matrix Σ contains the singular values that quantify the magnitude of variance along each principal direction, and the matrix V contains the principal components that define the spatial orientation of the learned task.

## 5 Evaluation Methodology and Experimental Results 

We evaluate continual learning along two complementary dimensions. First, we analyze temporal dynamics by tracking accuracy across epochs, comparing TORA against a continuous baseline (a single LoRA adapter without parameter isolation). This reveals when positive transfer (Boosting) occurs and confirms effective mitigation of catastrophic forgetting. Second, we assess robustness to task order via a Monte Carlo simulation [16] with N = 100 ,000 random task sequences, estimating long-term behavior under stochastic arrivals and the probability of transfer or isolation. Based on this, tasks are categorized as structural hubs that enable knowledge sharing or isolated nodes that require shielding.

6.1 Geometric Phase Transition and the Divergence Threshold 

The threshold ¯θ3 = 84 .5◦ emerges from the intrinsic geometry of the DistilBERT adapter space [1]. In high-dimensional spaces, orthogonal vectors ( 90 ◦) indicate complete independence between subspaces, a property that O-LoRA explicitly exploits by projecting new task updates onto the orthogonal complement of previous ones [23]. TORA operates on the same geometric principle, but uses the angular distance as a continuous compatibility measure rather than enforcing strict orthogonality. A cosine similarity of 84 .5◦ (≈ 0.095 ) marks the practical boundary where shared variance becomes too small to support positive transfer. Below this threshold, the gradient directions [17] of the incoming task align sufficiently with the prior expert to enable knowledge reuse. Above it, forcing Task-Oriented Rank Adaptation 13 

parameter transfer acts as a perturbation that requires the optimizer to unlearn irrelevant features, degrading performance. 

6.2 Structural Dynamics: Hubs vs. Isolated Nodes 

The routing decisions reveal two clearly distinct task roles. Broad datasets like DBPedia act as knowledge hubs, learning general geometric patterns that transfer easily to related domains and accelerate convergence. In contrast, tasks with strict objective functions such as MNLI, CB, and SST-2 develop incompatible parameter structures that require isolation. Forcing knowledge reuse in these cases causes geometric interference, confirming that the Shielding mechanism is essential to prevent negative transfer. 

6.3 Computational Efficiency and Limitations 

Beyond mitigating catastrophic forgetting, TORA reduces computational over-head by initializing LoRA modules with geometric aligned weights, allowing tasks to skip the initial exploratory phase of gradient descent and reach convergence faster, as shown by the epoch savings reported in Table 5. However, the current system uses a fixed neighborhood size k = 3 and a static divergence threshold, which may limit scalability in longer task sequences. As the number of experts grows, a static k risks capturing overlapping or contradictory gradient directions. Future work should explore dynamic neighborhood selection, such as adaptive clustering or density-based routing, to maintain transfer quality as the expert pool scales to 50 or more sequential tasks. 

## 7 Conclusion 

This work presented TORA, a geometric routing framework for continual learning in text classification that decides whether to transfer or isolate knowledge based solely on the angular distance between task fingerprints in the SVD parameter space. Evaluated across 15 diverse benchmarks, TORA never induced a harmful routing decision: compatible tasks benefited from the Boosting protocol, saving up to 10 training epochs while exceeding isolated baselines, and structurally distant tasks were protected by Shielding , preventing catastrophic forgetting without any accuracy loss. With a single geometric threshold of ¯θ3 = 84 .5◦ and no reliance on task identities or predefined sequences, TORA demonstrates that the intrinsic geometry of adapter weights is a sufficient and reliable signal for autonomous knowledge routing in sequential text classification systems.
