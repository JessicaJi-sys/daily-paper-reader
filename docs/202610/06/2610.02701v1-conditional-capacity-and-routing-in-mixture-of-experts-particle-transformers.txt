# Abstract 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models can increase parameter capacity without propor-tionally increasing active computation, but it is unclear how this trade-off behaves in particle-physics transformers. We study dense and MoE Particle Transform-ers on 188-class JetClass-II, varying expert count, routing capacity, top-K, and auxiliary loss. We find that, when token dropping is avoided, top-1 MoE models improve over the dense baseline at nearly unchanged nominal forward compute, while further increasing the number of stored experts produces little additional accuracy gain. Activating multiple experts per token yields additional predictive improvements at higher computational cost. Routing analyses show that expert assignments become more strongly associated with particle identity and kinematics in some configurations, but this structure does not increase monotonically with classification performance. These results highlight the need to distinguish stored parameter capacity, active computation, routing capacity, and routing organization when evaluating sparse expert models for jet classification. Code and experiment configurations are available at https://github.com/kpendiyala/MPT .

# 1 Introduction 

Particle Transformer (ParT) incorporates pairwise particle information into attention for jet classifica-tion [Qu et al., 2022]. Extending classification to the diverse resonance signatures in JetClass-II [Li et al., 2024] raises the question of how to allocate model capacity. Dense scaling increases parameters and computation together. Sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) layers select a few expert networks per token, allowing parameter growth without proportional growth in active computation [Shazeer et al., 2017, Fedus et al., 2021]. However, this does not guarantee better predictions. Restricted expert capacity can cause token assignments to be dropped, while more active experts change both computation and expert output combinations. Simultaneously, routing according to identifiable particle properties does not neces-sarily improve whole-jet classification. We therefore evaluate predictive performance together with routing behavior to determine whether more structured routing accompanies improved classification. We compare dense ParT and MoEParT across expert count, capacity, top-K, and auxiliary loss. We find that no-drop top-1 expert scaling changes routing without improving accuracy, whereas top-2 improves prediction at higher nominal compute. Capacity constraints accompany degraded performance, and stronger routing association is not a monotonic indicator of better classification.

# 2 Related Work 

Deep learning for jet tagging. Modern jet-tagging methods increasingly operate directly on sets of reconstructed particles rather than relying only on engineered jet observables. ParticleNet represents jets as particle clouds and applies dynamic graph convolution, providing a strong benchmark for constituent-based jet classification [Qu and Gouskos, 2020]. Particle Transformer (ParT) subsequently introduced a Transformer architecture that incorporates pairwise particle interactions into the attention mechanism and achieved improved performance on the large-scale JetClass benchmark [Qu et al., 2022]. Our work retains the ParT representation and attention structure while modifying the feed-forward sublayers through sparse mixture-of-experts routing. 

Large-scale jet pre-training and JetClass-II. JetClass-II extends large-scale jet classification to a substantially broader set of boosted-jet signatures. The Sophon framework uses a Particle Transformer pre-trained on a 188-class JetClass-II classification task and demonstrates the utility of the learned representation for heavy-resonance searches and transfer learning [Li et al., 2024]. In contrast, our study uses the JetClass-II classification task as a controlled setting for comparing dense and sparse-expert Particle Transformers, focusing on conditional parameter capacity, routing capacity, active computation, and routing organization rather than on downstream transfer. 

Sparse mixture-of-experts models. Mixture-of-experts models use learned routing to activate only a subset of model parameters for each input, increasing stored parameter capacity without requiring all parameters to be evaluated for every token. Early sparsely gated MoE models demonstrated large increases in parameter capacity through conditional computation [Shazeer et al., 2017]. GShard extended sparse expert routing to large Transformer models and distributed training [Lepikhin et al., 2021], while Switch Transformers simplified routing to a single active expert and emphasized expert-capacity and load-balancing issues [Fedus et al., 2021]. Subsequent work such as ST-MoE further studied training stability and transfer behavior in sparse expert Transformers [Zoph et al., 2022]. These studies motivate our separation of stored expert capacity, active expert computation, and routing capacity when evaluating MoEParT. 

Mixture-of-experts models in high-energy physics. Mixture-of-experts architectures have also begun to appear in high-energy physics. Genovese et al. combine graph Transformers with MoE layers for event-level signal-background classification, emphasizing expert specialization and interpretability [Genovese et al., 2025]. Our setting differs in that we study sparse expert routing within a Particle Transformer operating on constituent-level jet representations. To our knowledge, prior work has not systematically studied sparse MoE routing within the Particle Transformer architecture for large-scale jet classification.

Task and samples. We study 188-class jet classification on the Pythia JetClass-II dataset. The labels consist of two-prong resonances (0–14), three- and four-prong resonances (15–160), and QCD backgrounds (161–187). Each jet is represented by up to 128 particles using kinematic, 3particle-identity, charge, and displacement features, with preprocessing defined by the JetClass-II configuration. Final predictive performance is evaluated on a held-out test subset containing 456,822 selected jets, of which 353,206 are signal and 103,616 are QCD. Checkpoints are selected using validation accuracy, while routing and representation analyses are performed on validation data to avoid using the held-out test set for model interpretation. All reported test metrics use the frozen selected checkpoint for each configuration. 

Training setup. All dense and MoE configurations were trained using the same JetClass-II training and validation subsets and matched optimization budgets. The training subset consisted of 30 Parquet files: 10 each from the Res2P, Res34P, and QCD samples, corresponding to 3.0 million raw jets before the JetClass-II event selection. The validation subset consisted of six files, two from each sample group, corresponding to 600,000 raw jets before selection. Models were trained for 20 epochs with 1,000,000 sampled training examples per epoch and 600,000 validation examples per epoch, using a batch size of 512. Optimization used Ranger with an initial learning rate of 5 × 10 −4. All model configurations used the same training files, validation files, samples per epoch, epoch count, batch size, and optimization settings, so comparisons between dense and MoE configurations use matched data exposure and training budgets. Checkpoints were selected according to validation accuracy. Each reported configuration corresponds to a single training run; variation across random seeds was not evaluated. 

Experimental configurations. We compare dense ParT with four MoE studies: (i) capacity-limited top-1 models with E ∈ { 2, 4, 8} and c = 1 .5; (ii) no-drop top-1 models with c = E; (iii) top-K

models (E, K, c ) = (4 , 2, 2) , (8 , 2, 4) , (8 , 4, 2) ; and (iv) an auxiliary-loss sweep at (E, K, c ) = (4 , 1, 4) with α ∈ { 0, 0.01 , 0.05 }. Unless stated otherwise, α = 0 .01 ; this checkpoint is shared with the no-drop E4/K1 model.

Additional stored experts show diminishing accuracy gains. Within the no-drop K = 1 sweep, increasing E from 2 to 8 raises the parameter count from 3.64M to 11.62M while leaving active FLOPs essentially unchanged, yet accuracy remains nearly flat. Thus, the gain from sparse conditional capacity appears to saturate quickly in accuracy: E4 and E8 do not improve on E2 despite substantially greater stored capacity. The picture is more nuanced for background rejection, where R50 increases from 83.03 to 88.71, indicating that additional experts can still alter discrimination behavior even when top-line multiclass accuracy has saturated. 

Routing capacity confounds expert-count scaling. The behavior of the expert-count sweep changes substantially when routing capacity is constrained. With a fixed capacity factor c = 1 .5, the fraction of dropped valid assignments grows rapidly with expert count, reaching 21 .1% for E = 8 

(Fig. 3(a)). Performance deteriorates in the same regime: the capacity-limited E8 model reaches 

39 .90% accuracy, compared with 42 .73% when sufficient capacity eliminates dropping, while R50 

increases from 54 .53 to 88 .71 . Similar, though smaller, recoveries occur for E2 and E4. Thus, comparisons across expert count can be strongly confounded by routing capacity; increasing E

without sufficient capacity simultaneously changes the available parameter capacity and the fraction of assignments that are successfully processed. Because the capacity-limited and no-drop models are separately trained, these results establish an association between overflow and degradation rather than a causal effect of token dropping alone. 

More active experts improve performance at higher compute. Increasing K from 1 to 2 improves accuracy for both E4 and E8, but requires roughly 51% more nominal forward FLOPs (Fig. 3(b)). Routing therefore characterizes how representations are partitioned across experts, but does not by itself explain predictive quality. 

Auxiliary loss has metric-dependent effects. At fixed E4/K1, varying α changes QCD rejection and routing association without a monotonic accuracy trend. In particular, α = 0 .05 improves rejection while producing lower first-block particle-type NMI, indicating that stronger load balancing does not consistently improve either accuracy or routing structure. 

# 5 Discussion and conclusion 

Our results show that mixture-of-experts Particle Transformers should not be characterized by expert count alone. Under top-1 routing, increasing the number of stored experts expands conditional parameter capacity while leaving active computation nearly unchanged, yet the corresponding predic-tive gains saturate quickly. In contrast, activating multiple experts per token improves performance while increasing active computation. These behaviors separate two distinct forms of MoE scaling: increasing the parameters available to the router and increasing the amount of expert computation applied to each token. Routing capacity is also essential to this comparison. Increasing expert count without sufficient assignment capacity can cause a growing fraction of routed tokens to be dropped, confounding the effect of additional model capacity with reduced effective computation. Meaningful comparisons between sparse expert models therefore require considering not only how many experts are available, but also whether the routing mechanism can make use of them. The routing analysis provides a complementary perspective. Expert assignments develop measurable associations with particle identity and kinematic properties, showing that the router learns structured partitions of particle representations. However, stronger routing structure does not monotonically correspond to higher classification performance. Routing organization is therefore informative about how the model allocates representations across experts, but should not be treated as a direct proxy for predictive quality or semantic expert specialization. 6Overall, the effectiveness of MoEParT depends on the interaction between stored parameter capacity, active computation, and routing capacity. Sparse conditional computation can improve over a dense Particle Transformer at comparable nominal forward compute, but simply adding more experts is not sufficient; how those experts are activated and whether routed assignments are successfully processed are equally important. 

# 6 Limitations and Future Work 

This study focuses on a single Particle Transformer backbone and JetClass-II training setup, so the observed trade-offs may not transfer unchanged to other architectures or jet-classification tasks. In addition, the reported configurations are currently represented by a single trained checkpoint, leaving variation across random seeds unmeasured. The current study also uses a restricted training subset relative to the full JetClass-II training sample, which limits conclusions about how sparse expert capacity behaves in a higher-data regime. Future work will therefore evaluate the principal dense and MoE configurations across multiple seeds and larger training subsets to determine the stability of the observed performance and routing trends. Beyond addressing these scope limitations, several extensions could help clarify when and why sparse expert routing is useful. One direction is to study data scaling explicitly, comparing dense and MoE models as the available training sample increases to determine whether additional expert capacity becomes more valuable when data is less limiting. Another is to investigate routing dynamics during training rather than only at the selected checkpoint, which could reveal when expert specialization emerges and whether routing structure stabilizes, collapses, or reorganizes over time.
