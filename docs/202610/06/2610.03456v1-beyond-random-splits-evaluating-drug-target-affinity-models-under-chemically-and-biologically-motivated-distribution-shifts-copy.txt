# Beyond Random Splits: Evaluating Drug-Target Affinity Models Under Chemically and Biologically Motivated Distribution Shifts 

Minjae Chung, Clara Li, Malar Paavai Muthukumaran, Shaunna Wang, Aniket Ramkrishnan Iyer, Shaun Qien Yeau Tan, Harinishree Sathu, Micky C. Nnamdi, J. Ben Tamo, Benoit Louis Marteau, May Dongmei Wang

## Abstract 

Drug-target affinity (DTA) prediction is widely used to prioritize candidate com-pounds before costly experimental screening. DTA models are often compared under a single data split, even though deployment may require extrapolation to new chemical series, new protein targets, or both. We ask whether the distribu-tion shift used for evaluation changes which architecture appears best. We curate 718,800 unique drug-protein pairs from the ChEMBL and BindingDB datasets. We compare a Morgan-fingerprint + protein-CNN baseline with 12 controlled architectures that combine four drug representations with three ESM-2 interaction modes. Mean validation RMSE increases from 0.950 and 0.945 under scaffold and fingerprint-cluster OOD to 1.299 and 1.321 under protein-cluster and dual OOD. Model rankings are similar across the two chemical shifts ( τ = 0 .79 ), but agreement with scaffold OOD falls under protein OOD ( τ = 0 .39 ) and reverses under dual OOD ( τ = −0.55 ). Held-out evaluation, repeated seeds, group-aware bootstrap analysis, and a size-matched control support the same conclusion: archi-tecture selection depends on the form of extrapolation, not only on average error or training-set size. DTA benchmarks should therefore match the chemical and target shifts expected at deployment. 

## 1 Introduction 

Drug-target affinity (DTA) prediction estimates the strength of interaction between a small molecule and a protein target and is widely used to prioritize compounds before costly experimental screening. Early DTA architectures used task-specific sequence and graph encoders (Nguyen et al. [2021], Huang et al. [2021]). More recent models combine fixed chemical descriptors or learned molecular encoders with pretrained protein language models and cross-modal interaction mechanisms (Lin et al. [2023], Lu et al. [2025], Jiang et al. [2026], Bidgoli et al. [2026]). These approaches differ in how they represent chemical substructure, protein context, and drug-protein complementarity, making architecture choice an important practical decision. Drug discovery poses distinct extrapolation problems. In lead optimization and virtual screening, models trained on known binders must score compounds outside the chemical neighborhoods rep-resented in training, including unseen scaffolds and structurally dissimilar molecules. In target identification and drug repurposing, the protein itself may have no close sequence homolog in the labeled training data. These are distinct extrapolation tasks. The first requires transfer of learned structure-activity relationships across chemical space, while the second requires transfer of interaction rules across sequence space. Prior work often describes these as unseen-compound and unseen-target “cold-start” settings, in contrast to “warm-start” evaluation where both entities are represented during training (Lu et al. [2025], Jiang et al. [2026]).

Evaluation protocols can obscure these deployment-relevant differences. Random pair-level splitting can place structurally similar compounds or closely homologous proteins on both sides of the train-test boundary and has been shown to give optimistic estimates of DTA and drug–target interaction (DTI) generalization (Bai et al. [2021]). Recent work has likewise emphasized dataset bias, target leakage, and cross-domain performance drops (Özçelik et al. [2023], Avdiunina et al. [2025]). At the same time, the canonical Davis and KIBA benchmarks, which contain approximately 30k and 118k kinase-focused interactions, limit the diversity of the chemical and target space represented in many architecture comparisons (Jiang et al. [2026], Bidgoli et al. [2026], Davis et al. [2011], Tang et al. [2014]). Larger curated resources such as Papyrus and BindingDB-derived benchmarks broaden this coverage, but make harmonization and split construction central to how generalization is measured (Béquignon et al. [2023], Khokhlov et al. [2025]). This motivates evaluating architectural designs on a larger, heterogeneous affinity collection under explicitly separated chemical- and target-shift regimes. Prior work has evaluated chemical- and target-held-out settings, but these studies typically use them to assess a proposed model rather than to ask whether architecture rankings themselves remain stable across evaluation regimes. We address this question using 718,800 drug-protein pairs from ChEMBL and BindingDB (Zdrazil et al. [2024], Liu et al. [2007]). Our main contributions are: • To our knowledge, we provide the first systematic DTA evaluation framework centered on architecture-selection stability across complementary scaffold, chemical-similarity, target-sequence, and joint chemical-target distribution shifts. The four regimes are constructed within a common curated benchmark so that differences in model preference can be attributed to the form of extrapolation being tested. • We conduct a controlled factorial comparison of DTA architecture design choices. 

We evaluate 12 models spanning four drug representations and three ESM-2 interaction modes, together with a Morgan-fingerprint + protein-CNN baseline. We compare the drug representation and interaction strategy under identical evaluation regimes. • We show that the evaluation regime changes model-selection conclusions, not only prediction error. Architecture rankings remain similar across the two chemical shifts but reorganize under protein and dual OOD, with this pattern supported by held-out evaluation, repeated seeds, Kendall and Spearman rank analyses, group-aware bootstrap uncertainty, split-severity analysis, and a size-matched control. 

## 2 Methodology 

2.1 Dataset 

We combined inhibition constant ( Ki) measurements from ChEMBL and BindingDB, two large public databases of experimentally measured small-molecule–protein bioactivity and binding-affinity data, and converted them to pK i = − log 10 (Ki[M]) . Rows with invalid molecular structures, missing protein sequences, or missing affinity values were removed. Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) strings were standardized with RDKit, a toolkit used to analyze and manipulate 2chemical data. Repeated measurements for the same drug-protein pair were then identified and mean-aggregated before split construction. The final dataset contains 718,800 unique drug-protein pairs , spanning 273,528 compounds and 

3,704 proteins . Median pK i is 6.48 (mean 6.61, SD 1.52), with 616,359 pairs (85.7%) satisfying 

pK i > 5 (Ki < 10 μM). 

2.2 Evaluation regimes 

We evaluate model generalization under four increasingly challenging out-of-distribution (OOD) regimes that hold out chemical structure, chemical similarity, protein sequence similarity, or both drug and protein distributions. 

Scaffold OOD. Bemis-Murcko scaffolds are computed using RDKit and assigned group-wise to train, validation, or test partitions. 

Fingerprint-cluster OOD. Compounds are represented using 2048-bit extended-connectivity fin-gerprints with a maximum diameter of 4 bonds (ECFP4) . [Bai et al., 2021; Simm et al., 2021] :

.Sequential sphere-exclusion clustering uses a Tanimoto similarity threshold of 0.60, with each com-pound joining the first qualifying center or seeding a new cluster. Entire clusters are assigned to one partition. 

Protein-cluster OOD. Protein sequences are represented by amino-acid 3-mer sets and clustered using Jaccard similarity at threshold 0.40. Each protein cluster is assigned entirely to one partition. 

Dual OOD. Chemical groups and protein clusters are first assigned independently to train, validation, or test. We retain an interaction only when both assignments agree. A test interaction therefore contains a drug from a held-out chemical group and a protein from a held-out sequence cluster, so neither side of the pair is represented in training. This directly tests simultaneous extrapolation across both axes. Conflicting assignments are removed, which reduces the retained training set. Section B.3 tests whether that reduction alone explains the ranking change. 

2.3 Architecture matrix and training 

We first evaluate a Morgan-fingerprint + character-level protein Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) baseline under all four split definitions. This provides a non-pretrained protein encoder outside the Evolutionary Scale Modeling-2 (ESM-2) design space. The controlled matrix then contains 12 architectures formed from four drug representations and three protein interaction modes. The drug representations are ECFP4, ChemBERTa, GCN, and fused GCN+ChemBERTa. All three protein modes use ESM-2 and differ in aggregation through mean pooling, learned attention pooling, or drug-conditioned cross-attention. This 4 × 3 design separates drug representation choice from interaction strategy under a common evaluation protocol. Architectures are trained independently within each regime and ranked by RMSE; ranking agreement is measured primarily with Kendall’s τ . Spearman’s rank correlation ( ρ) is additionally used as a secondary validation of rank stability across evaluation regimes. Split-severity statistics, optimization settings, sample-size controls, rank-correlation analyses, and architecture-specific training details are reported in Appendix A. 

## 3 Results 

3.1 Distribution shift dominates absolute error 

Across the 12 architectures, performance is similar under the two drug-based distribution shifts. Mean validation RMSE is slightly lower under fingerprint OOD than scaffold OOD (0.945 vs. 0.950) despite fingerprint OOD having lower chemical similarity to the training set. Error increases substantially when generalizing to unseen proteins: mean RMSE rises to 1.299 under protein OOD and 1.321 when both drugs and proteins are OOD, corresponding to increases of 36.7% and 39.0% relative to scaffold OOD. These results identify target-sequence shift as the dominant difficulty increase in the current bench-mark. Dual OOD is hardest overall, but only modestly exceeds protein OOD despite adding substantial chemical separation. Held-out evaluation of all 12 architectures preserves this severity pattern, with a mean RMSE of 0.986, 0.946, 1.354, and 1.368 under scaffold, fingerprint, protein, and dual OOD, respectively (Table 6). The split-specific winner is unchanged for scaffold, fingerprint, and dual OOD, while protein OOD changes from ECFP+AttnPool on validation to GCN+ChemBERTa+CrossAttn on the held-out test set. 

3.2 Architecture rankings depend on the regime 

Scaffold and fingerprint OOD produce similar model orderings ( τ = 0 .79 , 7 of 66 pairwise reversals). Agreement falls between scaffold and protein OOD ( τ = 0 .39 , 20 reversals) and becomes negative between scaffold and dual OOD ( τ = −0.55 , 51 reversals). Fingerprint versus dual OOD shows the largest inversion ( τ = −0.64 , 54 reversals). The top three architectures under scaffold and dual OOD do not overlap. Spearman rank correlations independently reproduce this pattern, with strong agreement between scaffold and fingerprint OOD ( ρ = 0 .90 ) and negative correlations between the chemical and dual-OOD rankings ( ρ = −0.73 for scaffold and −0.77 for fingerprint). Full pairwise results are provided in Appendix A.3. The clearest inversion is between ECFP+AttnPool and GCN+CrossAttn. ECFP+AttnPool ranks first under scaffold, fingerprint, and protein OOD but last under dual OOD. GCN+CrossAttn ranks near the bottom in the first three regimes and first under dual OOD. Their relative advantage changes from 0.104 RMSE in favor of ECFP+AttnPool under scaffold OOD to 0.092 in favor of GCN+CrossAttn under dual OOD (Figure 2). Target-sequence shift produces the largest increase in prediction error in this benchmark. Moving from chemical OOD to protein OOD increases average RMSE substantially more than changing between scaffold- and fingerprint-based chemical shifts. Architecture preference also changes across regimes. The Morgan-fingerprint + protein-CNN baseline shows the same regime dependence outside the controlled ESM-2 matrix, ranking lowest on the chemical-OOD validation splits, highest under protein OOD, and lowest again under dual OOD. The strongest reversal occurs under dual OOD. Repeated initialization seeds preserve its direction. Five size-matched protein-OOD subsets do not reproduce the GCN advantage, while group-aware bootstrap intervals favor GCN under dual OOD. Together with the split-severity audit, these controls support an architecture-specific response to simultaneous chemical and target-sequence shift rather than a simple training-size effect. This pattern is consistent with different architectural inductive biases becoming useful under different forms of extrapolation. These findings also suggest a potential mitigation through similarity-aware model selection, where nearest-training chemical similarity (ECFP4 Tanimoto) and target-sequence similarity (3-mer Jaccard) are estimated before prediction and used to select among architectures with different OOD strengths. In our benchmark, dual OOD is characterized by low similarity on both axes (median Tanimoto 0.619; median Jaccard 0.142), whereas protein OOD retains chemically familiar compounds (median Tanimoto 1.000) while shifting target sequences (median Jaccard 0.139), suggesting that these similarity scores could serve as routing signals for selecting models suited to the expected form of extrapolation. This distinction matters for model selection in drug discovery. A benchmark that preserves familiar compounds but holds out target neighborhoods asks a different scientific question from one that simultaneously moves into new chemical and target space. Reporting a single aggregate benchmark number can therefore obscure which inductive biases are favored by the intended deployment setting. Our results motivate evaluating DTA models under explicitly characterized chemical and target shifts and reporting ranking stability, not only absolute error. The study remains retrospective and sequence-based. Public ChEMBL and BindingDB measurements combine heterogeneous assay contexts, and the present split definitions remain proxies for prospective discovery. ## A Detailed study design 

A.1 Split-severity audit 

Nearest-training similarity is measured after partitioning. On the chemical axis, median nearest-training ECFP4 Tanimoto is 0.719 for scaffold OOD, 0.676 for fingerprint OOD, 1.000 for protein OOD, and 0.619 for dual OOD. Thus, the protein split leaves the compound side familiar, while dual OOD adds a substantial chemical-space shift. On the target axis, scaffold and fingerprint splits have a median nearest-training 3-mer Jaccard of 1.00, whereas protein and dual OOD reduce this value to 0.139 and 0.142. Approximately 32% of test proteins in both protein-focused regimes have nearest-training Jaccard below 0.10.

A.2 Training and analysis details 

Architectures are trained independently within each regime and ranked by RMSE. Rank agreement between split pairs is quantified with Kendall’s τ . With 12 architectures, each ranking contains 66 architecture pairs. Three representative architectures are retrained across three random seeds. The training-size control downsamples the protein-OOD training partition from approximately 511k to 352k pairs to match the dual-OOD training size. 7All models are optimized with AdamW using weight decay 1 × 10 −4 and cosine annealing, with a maximum of 40 epochs and early-stopping patience of 7 epochs based on RMSE. Learning rates are 1 × 10 −3 for ECFP/GCN branches and 5 × 10 −4 for branches containing ChemBERTa. ESM-2 ( esm2_t12_35M_UR50D , 480 dimensions) and ChemBERTa ( DeepChem/ChemBERTa-77M-MTR ,384 dimensions) are frozen during training. Only task-specific graph, projection, interaction, and regression modules are optimized. Architecture-specific batch sizes range from 64 to 2048, and trainable parameter counts range from 0.62M to 2.09M (Table 4). 

A.3 Rank-correlation analysis 

As a secondary check of ranking stability, we computed Spearman’s rank correlation ( ρ) between the architecture rankings induced by each pair of evaluation regimes. The results reproduce the pattern observed with Kendall’s τ . Scaffold and fingerprint OOD show strong agreement ( ρ = 0 .895 ), whereas agreement weakens when either chemical regime is compared with protein OOD ( ρ = 0 .448 

and 0.434 ). Rankings become negatively correlated under dual OOD, with ρ = −0.727 relative to scaffold OOD and ρ = −0.769 relative to fingerprint OOD. Protein and dual OOD are also negatively correlated ( ρ = −0.476 ). Thus, the observed ranking reorganization is not specific to the choice of Kendall’s τ as the primary rank-agreement statistic.
