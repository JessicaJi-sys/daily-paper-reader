Abstract 

Deep graph learning models deployed in real-world systems often need to cope with non-stationary environments, where the underlying graph distribution drifts continually over time. Prevailing solutions rely on training auxiliary generative modules to synthesize memory graphs for cross-domain adaptation, which incurs substantial computational overhead and scales poorly under prolonged distribu-tion shifts. We argue that a more economical path exists: rather than generating memory, one can crystallize it. To this end, we propose Efficient Memory Crys-tallization (EMC), a training-free test-time framework that distills each incoming graph domain into a compact, semantically faithful memory through a closed-form solution to a memory-oriented distribution-matching objective, thereby eliminat-ing redundant domain information under continual covariate shifts. To preserve both generalizability and adaptability as the model traverses a long sequence of target domains, EMC further models inter-domain dependencies through state-evolving memories and admits a theoretically grounded, tighter generalization error bound than direct adaptation. Extensive experiments demonstrate the superior performance of EMC over state-of-the-art baselines on graphs under non-stationary distribution shifts, while reducing average runtime by 87.4% and GPU memory consumption by 92.4% relative to the recent competitor, making continual graph adaptation practical at scale. 

1 Introduction 

Graph-structured data is ubiquitous in real-world applications such as social network analysis, recommender systems, and epidemiological modeling. To effectively learn from such data, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) [12, 37] have emerged as powerful models that integrate node features and structural information through neighborhood aggregation. While GNNs have demonstrated impressive performance under the assumption that training and testing data follow the same distribution, this assumption is often violated in practice due to distribution shift, that is, a mismatch between the training (source) and testing (target) distributions which poses a major challenge to the generalization ability of deep models. To bridge this gap, Graph Domain Adaptation (GDA) methods aim to transfer knowledge from the source domain to the target domain by mitigating the distributional discrepancy. In real-world scenarios, the source data is often inaccessible during test time due to privacy or business constraints, making the adaptation problem more challenging. Moreover, the GNN model frequently operates in non-stationary environments, where the target graph distribution drifts continually over time. Transferring knowledge from the source domain to entirely unseen target domains can introduce inductive bias, which exacerbates catastrophic forgetting and degrades the model’s generalization ability to future domains, especially when distribution shifts arrive in a continual stream rather than a

Recently, there has been a growing research trend focusing on single-step domain adapta-tion, typically employing techniques such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy [6] and adver-sarial learning [3, 25, 38]. However, these ap-proaches inevitably fall short when the model is exposed to distribution shifts that arrive con-tinually across time or domains. As graph data evolves, models encounter new domains that re-quire ongoing adaptation, while simultaneously losing the ability to perform well on previously seen graphs, which leads to catastrophic forget-ting. To mitigate catastrophic forgetting, Con-tinual Learning [30, 31, 39–41] has emerged as a promising direction. These methods typically adopt replay mechanisms, where the model peri-odically revisits selected or generated historical data to reinforce prior knowledge. Such strate-gies help balance training cost with long-term knowledge retention. However, most existing approaches rely on labeled data to guide mem-ory selection and replay, making them ill-suited for the test-time setting where labels are unavailable. Although GCAL [24] attempts to improve cross-domain adaptation by training an auxiliary generative module to synthesize memory graphs, its dependence on a separately trained generator introduces substantial computational overhead and limits its practicality under prolonged drift. Furthermore, as shown in Figure 1, current methods under test-time settings with continual drift often ignore the inter-domain relationships and the redundancy within each domain that is irrelevant to the under-lying semantic information. We summarize that a desirable framework for adapting graph models under non-stationary distribution shifts at test time should pursue three core objectives: ensuring the model’s generalizability , maintaining its adaptability to novel domains, and mitigating forgetting

2 Preliminaries 

Problem Setup. We consider the task of test-time adaptation on graphs under non-stationary distribution shifts. Given a GNN model f (G(s); Θ0) → Y that is pre-trained on one or multiple source graphs G(s) drawn from the source distribution PS (G, Y ), where Θ0 denotes the parameters of the pre-trained GNN encoder and {Y } ∈ Y is the label set. The target data is represented as a sequence of graphs T = {Gt}Tt=1 containing T test samples drawn from the marginalized target distribution. Each graph G(t) = {A(t), X(t)} ∈ PT (G) corresponds to the t-th target domain, where A(t) ∈ RNt×Nt is the adjacency matrix with Nt nodes, and X(t) ∈ RNt×d is the node feature with d-dimensional attributes. We use Gt to specifically denote the graph from the t-th target domain in the arrival order, and G(t) as a generic notation when the index (t) may refer to any past or current domain in {1, . . . , T }. With the covariate shift assumption, i.e., PS (G)̸ = PT (G)

and PS (Y |G) = PT (Y |G), we aim to continually adapt the model parameters from Θt−1 to Θt to maintain the generalizability and adaptability 2 on target samples from PT (G), while mitigating the risk of catastrophic forgetting on previously encountered domains. We summarize the frequently used notations in Appendix A. 

3 Methodology 

In this section, we elaborate on the proposed efficient test-time framework EMC with its overall pipeline shown in Figure 3. To efficiently extract low-dimensional representations with reduced redundancy within each domain, we first reformulate a memory-oriented distribution-matching objective and derive a closed-form solution that crystallizes each incoming domain into a compact, semantically faithful memory. To enable continual adaptation by capturing inter-domain relationships, we then propose state-evolving memories, which yield a tighter generalization error bound.

4 Experiments 

In this section, we empirically evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed EMC. 3 We organize the experiments around the following research questions: • RQ1: How well does EMC achieve generalizability, adaptability, and forgetting mitigation effi-ciently with low memory and computational cost under continual drift? • RQ2: How does EMC scale with respect to graph size and the number of domains? • RQ3: What are the impacts of different components on the effectiveness and efficiency of EMC? • RQ4: How sensitive is EMC to its hyperparameters?

We evaluate EMC on 4 node classification datasets, following the benchmark in GCAL [24], categorized into two types: • For regional shifts , the Facebook-100 [28] and Twitch-Explicit [26] datasets consist of multiple social network graphs collected from different regions. • For temporal shifts , OGB-Arxiv [9] is a citation network of academic papers, and Elliptic [23] is a Bitcoin transaction network, with both containing graphs spanning different time steps. 5 Related Work 

Graph Continual Learning. Graph continual learning aims to enable graph models to adapt to evolving data distributions while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Existing approaches are typically categorized into three major classes: regularization methods, parameter isolation methods, and memory replay methods. Regularization-based methods constrain weight updates to preserve important knowledge from previous tasks; for example, TWP [15] incorporates topology-aware weight preservation, and RieGrace [27] leverages gradient-based importance measures. Parameter isolation techniques, such as HPNs [39], allocate task-specific parameters to prevent interference between old and new knowledge. Memory replay methods alleviate forgetting by storing and reusing representative data, as seen in ER-GNN [44] and SSM [40]. While these methods are effective in continual learning scenarios, they generally assume access to labeled training data, limiting their applicability to real-world settings. Inspired by memory replay, our work eliminates the need for additional trainable components by reformulating the distribution matching objective and deriving a closed-form solution for crystallizing each incoming domain into a compact memory at test time. This makes our method well suited for the more realistic and challenging setting of graph learning under non-stationary distribution shifts at test time, where the input distribution drifts continually and no source data or annotations are available. 

Graph Domain Adaptation. Graph domain adaptation [19, 34, 36, 4, 16, 10] aims to transfer knowledge learned from labeled data in a source domain to different but related target domains. For example, UDA-GCN [33] and AdaGCN [2] enhance adaptability by exploiting graph topology and reducing source–target discrepancies through local/global consistency or domain adversarial objectives. Continual Test-Time Adaptation (CTTA) is a more practical extension of traditional Test-Time Adaptation (TTA), which tackles the challenge of adapting models to continuously drifting target domains. Recent CTTA approaches, such as CoTTA [31], incorporate strategies like bi-level pseudo-label averaging and stochastic model resets to improve robustness. The most recent graph-specific approach, GCAL [24], trains an auxiliary VGAE to generate memory graphs for replay, but the additional generative training is expensive and scales poorly under prolonged drift. However, existing methods largely overlook the continual nature of distribution shifts at test time and the redundancy within each domain that is irrelevant to the underlying semantics. In this paper, we explicitly model inter-domain relationships under continual drift through state-evolving memories and improve both the efficiency and effectiveness of adaptation by crystallizing each domain in closed form, without requiring any auxiliary trainable component at test time. 

6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present EMC (Efficient Memory Crystallization), a training-free test-time framework that crystallizes each incoming graph domain into a compact, semantically faithful memory through a closed-form solution, and models inter-domain dependencies via state-evolving memories with provably tighter generalization error bounds. Extensive experiments demonstrate the superiority of EMC, achieving substantial efficiency gains (87.4% less runtime and 92.4% less GPU memory on average over the recent baseline GCAL) while outperforming state-of-the-art baselines on graphs under non-stationary distribution shifts.

B.3 Semantic-Anchor Aggregation at Test Time 

We elaborate on how class-wise node partitioning in Section 3.1 is instantiated at test time, where ground-truth labels of target-domain graphs are unavailable, and explain why the resulting memories are robust to noisy partitioning. 

Test-Time Setting under Continual Drift. We consider a standard covariate shift setting, where the label space remains unchanged across domains, i.e. , PS (Y ) = PT (Y ) and PS (G)̸ = PT (G). That is, distribution shifts at test time affect the input graph distributions, while the semantic meanings of classes are consistent with those observed during pre-training. This assumption is critical: under covariate shift the class-conditional decision boundary is preserved across domains, so systematic confident misclassification by the current model is substantially less likely than under label shift. 

Semantic Anchors for Class-wise Aggregation. Class-wise partitioning at test time is guided by the model’s predicted semantics rather than ground-truth labels. Concretely, given a node v in the incoming graph G(t), the current model with parameters Θt−1 produces a predictive distribution 

pΘt−1 (y|v), from which a soft semantic assignment is derived together with a confidence score 

rv = max c∈Y pΘt−1 (y = c|v). Nodes with similar predicted semantics are grouped together, and the confidence score weights each node’s contribution to the resulting centroid through the aggregation matrix Ri in Eq. (1). Crucially, the predicted semantics serve as semantic anchors for grouping rather than as supervisory targets, so the procedure does not rely on the correctness of any individual assignment. 

Design Choices that Stabilize the Aggregation. Memory crystallization in EMC relies on four complementary design choices that together keep aggregation stable under continual drift: • Covariate shift limits systematic errors. As stated above, the preserved class-conditional decision boundary makes systematic confident misclassification far less likely than under label shift. • Anchors are not supervisory targets. They are used only to partition nodes into sub-clusters for centroid aggregation. Even noisy assignments yield valid centroids through a many-to-one smoothing effect, analogous to the behavior of k-means under individual point mislabeling [17]. • K-hop propagation denoises before partitioning. The features H = ˆAK X aggregate neighbor-hood information, smoothing out local prediction errors before memory construction. • Dirichlet regularization provides an independent smoothness constraint. The term α ·

tr( X′⊤ L′X′) in Eq. (6) enforces feature smoothness on the crystallized memory, further reducing any residual noise from partitioning. 17 Empirically, EMC consistently outperforms baselines on all four datasets ( ▷ Section 4.2), including Elliptic (temporal Bitcoin transaction shifts that can be abrupt) and OGB-Arxiv (year-by-year citation shifts), confirming that the four design choices above translate into reliable partitioning under continual drift in practice.

Interpretation of the Two Loss Terms. Proposition C.10 provides an explicit interpretation of the adaptation loss in Eq. (12) and clarifies how the two components in the overall objective serve complementary purposes during adaptation. • Adaptation to the current domain. The entropy minimization term H(Gt) encourages confident predictions on the current test graph. By reducing predictive uncertainty on Gt, this term drives the model to better align with the data distribution of the newly arriving domain. This component focuses exclusively on the current domain. • Stability on previously encountered domains. The KL divergence term DKL ( ˆ˜G(t)∥ ˜G(t)) does not compare different domains. Instead, for each previously stored memory ˜G(t), it constrains the change in predictive distributions induced by updating the model from Θt−1 to Θt. D Additional Experiments 

D.1 Dataset Description 

We evaluate EMC on four benchmark node classification datasets, encompassing two distinct types of distribution shifts: regional shifts and temporal shifts . This setting follows the continual adaptation protocol in GCAL [24], where each graph is treated as an individual domain. The statistical summary of all datasets is provided in Table 6. 

Regional Shifts. Facebook-100 [28] contains 100 ego-centric Facebook friendship networks collected from U.S. universities in 2005. Each network represents a unique university and varies significantly in node count, density, and degree distribution. We select three university graphs (Amherst41, Caltech36, and Johns Hopkins55) for initial training, and perform continual adaptation on eleven other university graphs without labels, following the protocol in [35]. Twitch-Explicit [26] includes seven social graphs of Twitch users from different regions (DE, ENGB, ES, FR, PTBR, RU, TW). Each graph contains users as nodes and friendship relations as edges. The DE network is used for pretraining, and the remaining regional graphs are used for continual test-time adaptation, also without labels. 

Temporal Shifts. OGB-Arxiv [9] is a citation network of Arxiv CS papers across 40 research areas. Nodes represent papers, and edges indicate citation links. We pretrain the model on papers published before 2011 and adapt it sequentially to papers published from 2011 onward, capturing the temporal evolution of scientific collaborations. Elliptic [23] consists of 49 graph snapshots of Bitcoin transaction networks. Nodes represent transactions, and edges denote payment flows. Approximately 20% of nodes are labeled as licit or illicit. To avoid extreme class imbalance, we use snapshots 7–9 for pretraining and adapt the model continually using the remaining graphs, reflecting the temporal dynamics of illicit activity detection. Across all datasets, we follow a consistent strategy: pretrain a GNN on a small set of labeled graphs, then continually adapt to the remaining unlabeled graphs in a test-time online setting. To simplify notation in our tables and figures, we refer to Twitch-Explicit as Twitch , Facebook-100 as Facebook ,and OGB-Arxiv as Arxiv .

D.3 Experimental Setting 

In this work, we are the first to investigate the generalizability and adaptability of graph models under non-stationary distribution shifts at test time, while also addressing the challenge of catastrophic forgetting. We distinguish between two key concepts: generalizability and adaptability . Generalizability refers to the model’s inherent ability to perform well on previously seen domains, even while being adapted to new domains. This emphasizes the model’s capacity to retain knowledge throughout the continual adaptation process. In contrast, adaptability reflects how effectively a model can enhance its performance on a newly encountered domain through test-time optimization using unlabeled data. In other words, generalizability focuses on performance retention, while adaptability emphasizes performance gain under novel domain shifts. Effectiveness across node-level and graph-level tasks. EMC attains the best Avg. on five out of eight (dataset, backbone) combinations and the runner-up on the remaining three, demonstrating consistent effectiveness beyond the node-level setting that the prior literature on graph continual test-time adaptation has primarily focused on. The pattern of best results also covers both task granularities: EMC leads on three of four node-level entries and on two of four graph-level entries, indicating that the closed-form memory crystallization transfers naturally from node-level to graph-level adaptation without any task-specific modification. We further note that EMC consistently achieves the best AF in most settings, confirming that the state-evolving design effectively mitigates forgetting under continual drift, whereas STEM’s stronger adaptation on certain domains is often accompanied by a negative AF ( e.g. , -3.92 on Mutagenicity-GraphSAGE, -4.40 on Arxiv-GraphSAGE), reflecting a less favorable retention–adaptation trade-off. 

Substantial Efficiency Advantage. The most pronounced gap appears in GPU memory consumption, where the closed-form construction in EMC removes the need for any auxiliary trainable component at test time. On node-level tasks, EMC is roughly an order of magnitude more memory-efficient than STEM and over an order of magnitude lighter than GCAL. The advantage widens on graph-level tasks, where EMC operates within a few hundred MiB while STEM exceeds ten thousand MiB on both Mutagenicity and FRANKENSTEIN, a difference of two orders of magnitude. Such a gap is structural rather than incidental: STEM and GCAL both rely on auxiliary modules whose memory cost grows with graph size, whereas the closed-form memory in EMC is solved analytically over a fraction of the input nodes ( ▷ Section 3.1), yielding a footprint that stays compact regardless of the underlying task granularity. These results confirm that EMC delivers competitive or superior effectiveness while remaining the most memory-efficient option among recent continual test-time adaptation methods, making it well-suited for prolonged adaptation under non-stationary shifts.

D.8 Robustness to Different Domain-Shift Patterns 

The state evolving field is a first-order linear interpolation, which prompts a natural question: does EMC remain effective when distribution shifts arrive in a non-monotonic or cyclic pattern? We note that Theorem 3.6 holds for any convex combination satisfying wt−1 + wt = 1 , so the theoretical tightness does not depend on a stronger linearity assumption. Empirically, the memory at each step is reconstructed from the current domain rather than carried forward, the state evolving field interpolates only encoded representations of adjacent domains, and entropy minimization keeps the model calibrated regardless of the order of arrival. We further verify this with two stress tests on Twitch-Explicit with GCN backbone: (i) varying the order of the six target domains to break monotonicity, and (ii) cyclic shifts where target domains repeat. Tables 8 and 9 show that EMC outperforms GCAL across all orderings and cyclic patterns, with much lower runtime and GPU memory. 

D.9 Memory Construction Alternatives 

To further verify that EMC’s efficiency advantage stems from both eliminating auxiliary generative training and producing informative memories, we compare against two simpler training-free memory schemes that replace GCAL’s VGAE: Random subgraph sampling and Feature averaging . As shown in Table 10, both alternatives achieve comparable cost to EMC but suffer substantial performance drops, with feature averaging falling below the base model. This confirms that the closed-form crystallization in EMC produces memories that are not only cheap but also semantically faithful.

Discussion on Memory Budgeting. A natural strategy is to cap the buffer at K memories and manage it as in agent-memory mechanisms, for example keeping the most recent ones in a first-in-first-out manner, retaining those most representative by similarity, or maintaining a weighted moving average of past memories. Because crystallization is a closed-form operation on node features, the oldest memories can also be merged and re-crystallized into a single memory at negligible cost, yielding constant storage, which is not available to generative replay without retraining the generator.

E Broader Impacts 

Distribution shifts arise pervasively in real-world graph applications, including social networks, recommendation systems, citation analysis, and financial transaction monitoring, where the underlying data evolves continually over time. By enabling graph models to adapt under such non-stationary shifts in a training-free and memory-efficient manner, EMC contributes to deploying reliable graph learning systems at lower computational and energy cost, which we view as a positive societal outcome. We do not foresee specific negative societal impacts beyond those general to applied machine learning. 

Limitations. Despite its practical advantages, EMC has two main limitations: • Fixed hyperparameter configuration. Our current experiments use a single fixed hyperparame-ter configuration for each dataset. The hyperparameters are not dynamically adjusted for each incoming domain, which may limit adaptation when the characteristics of distribution shifts vary substantially over time. • Lack of explicit drift measurement. EMC adapts sequentially under non-stationary distribution shifts, but does not explicitly estimate the magnitude of domain drift to determine the appropri-ate adaptation strength. Incorporating explicit drift estimation and drift-aware adaptation is a promising direction for future work. 32 Elliptic-29 Original Graph
