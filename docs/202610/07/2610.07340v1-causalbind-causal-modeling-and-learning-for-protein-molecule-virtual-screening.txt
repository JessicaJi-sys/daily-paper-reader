## Abstract 

Protein-molecule virtual screening is increasingly cast as a problem of represen-tation learning in a shared embedding space. Existing methods rely on dense holistic alignment, entangling invariant binding determinants with nuisance cor-relations and limiting transfer to new targets. It has been noted that binding in protein–molecule systems involves sparse cross-modality interactions: binding is governed by a small contact interface and a few decisive local interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and salt bridges) rather than the global structures of the protein and molecule. We hypothesize that uncovering and leverag-ing sparse interaction patterns is critical for generalization beyond the training data, as these patterns are reusable and expected to improve performance across different scenarios. In this paper, we aim to identify and leverage sparse interaction patterns, and verify our hypothesis. Since the training data contain only observed binding pairs, we formalize this prior via a V-structure causal model under Heckman-style selection, and establish three theoretical results: (i) the latent concepts of inter-acting proteins and molecules are not identifiable without appropriate sparsity constraints; (ii) these concepts and their sparse interactions are component-wise identifiable under structural sparsity conditions; and (iii) a low-rank relaxation of these conditions yields subspace identifiability of the concepts and interactions. Inspired by these principles, we propose C AUSAL BIND with three implementation variants. Extensive experiments on DUD-E and LIT-PCBA benchmarks show that all variants consistently outperform strong retrieval baselines, with the largest gains on LIT-PCBA early enrichment, and further generalize to target- and scaffold-level out-of-distribution splits. Code and scripts to reproduce our experiments are publicly available from GitHub at https://github.com/lokali/CausalBind .

## 1 Introduction 

Modern drug discovery faces a fundamental challenge: identifying promising drug candidates from libraries containing millions to billions of candidate compounds [Berdigaliyev and Aljofan, 2020]. Virtual screening [Walters and Wang, 2020], which prioritizes protein-molecule pairs before costly experimental validation, has become a major bottleneck in this pipeline and a central problem in AI for drug discovery [Jia et al., 2026]. An overview of the broader pipeline is illustrated in App. A1. Existing virtual-screening methods broadly fall into three regimes: physics-based docking sys-tems [Friesner et al., 2004, Trott and Olson, 2010] that score complexes via force fields; structure-aware machine-learning scoring models [McNutt et al., 2021, Shen et al., 2022, 2023, Cao et al., 2024, Moon et al., 2022, Lu et al., 2022, Brocidiacono et al., 2023] that learn to rank protein-molecule complexes from data; and retrieval-based representation learners [Gao et al., 2023, Han et al., 2025, Feng et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2026, He et al., 2026] that embed both modalities into a shared space. The first two reason explicitly about three-dimensional poses and are valuable for mechanistic analysis, but they are typically better suited to rescoring a small candidate set than to ultra-large-scale

Despite this success, retrieval models still learn holistic representations: they align full molecule and protein embeddings without separating binding-relevant determinants from nuisance factors. This reflects a structural mismatch with the biological process, as illustrated in Figure 1, where the binding decision is governed by a small contact interface and a few decisive local interactions rather than the global structures of the protein and molecule. A classical example is the notion of “hot spots”, where a small subset of interfacial contacts contributes the majority of binding free energy [Clackson and Wells, 1995, Bogan and Thorn, 1998]. As a result, holistic models are prone to capturing spurious global correlations rather than the local structural features that actually drive binding [Chen et al., 2019, Graber et al., 2025], which may hinder generalization to novel protein families and unseen chemical scaffolds. Additional details about biological observations are provided in App. A3. These limitations motivate the core question to be answered in this paper: if protein-molecule binding is driven by a small number of local compatibilities, should one represent virtual-screening in terms of the sparse concept-level interactions, instead of potentially dense holistic alignment, and leverage this representation for better generalization ability? Our results strongly suggest that the answer is yes. To analyze this issue, we first develop a V-structure causal model of binding under Heckman-style selection [Schölkopf et al., 2021, Zhang et al., 2026], and establish three identifiability results for the sparse protein-molecule interaction: dense cross-modal interaction is generally non-identifiable, sparse antichain interaction is component-wise identifiable up to trivial equivalences, and under a low-rank condition the interaction modes are subspace-identifiable. Building on this theory, we propose C AUSAL BIND as a flexible structure-constrained representation framework with three different implementations: the main C AUSAL BIND SP uses a concept extractor and a learnable sparse concept-pair mask , C AUSAL BIND LR uses a low-rank factorization of the same concept-axis mask, and C AUSAL BIND EMB applies the constrained mask after pooling in embedding space. Our main technical contributions are threefold. (i) Theoretically, we provide an identifiability analysis of representation learning for protein-molecule binding under binder-only selection, show-ing that dense interactions are not identifiable, whereas sparse antichain-structured interactions are component-wise identifiable, and low-rank interactions are subspace-identifiable. (ii) Method-ologically, we instantiate these principles through three C AUSAL BIND implementations within a concept-based retrieval framework. C AUSAL BIND SP routes binding evidence through a learnable sparse concept-pair mask, C AUSAL BIND LR imposes an explicit low-rank concept-axis factorization, and C AUSAL BIND EMB applies the constraint in pooled embedding space. (iii) Empirically, on DUD-E and LIT-PCBA benchmarks, all three C AUSAL BIND implementations outperform repro-duced baseline methods, with the largest relative gains on the property-debiased LIT-PCBA early enrichment. Additional analyses, including multi-seed ablations, hyperparameter studies, antichain 2diagnostics of the learned masks, out-of-distribution evaluation on DEKOIS 2.0, an interpretability case study, and affinity-ranking results on additional benchmarks, support the efficacy of our method.

In § 2.1, we model binding as a V-structure and study identifiability [Daunhawer et al., 2023] under Heckman-style selection [Heckman, 1979]. Our theorems in § 2.2 proceed in three phases, from non-identifiability to component-wise and subspace identifiability.

Assumption 1 (Concept recoverability) . The latent concepts cM and cP can be written as functions of the corresponding observations xM and xP , respectively. 

Assumption 2 (Prior independence) . Before conditioning on B, the two modalities are independent, i.e., p(cM , cP ) = p(cM )p(cP ).

Assumption 3 (Positivity / coverage) . We have P (B=1) > 0, and every atomic concept appears with positive probability under the binder-selected distribution pD .

In Definition 1, component-wise identifiability is stronger because it recovers each concept, whereas subspace identifiability recovers only a low-dimensional subspace spanned by several concepts. Assumptions 1–3 are shared by subsequent theorems. They ensure that (i) concept information is preserved from the observed modalities, (ii) cross-modal dependence arises only through selection, and (iii) sufficient coverage is available for recovery, respectively. 2.2 Identifiability Results 

We next characterize, in a progressively structured way, when the latent interaction mechanism is identifiable from binder-selected data. First, binder-only selection by itself is insufficient: without suitable structural constraints, interacting protein and molecule concepts are not identifiable. Second, such concepts and sparse interactions are component-wise identifiable under proper structural sparsity conditions. Third, low-rank interaction structure yields subspace identifiability when only low-dimensional interaction subspaces can be determined. We give the three formal statements below.

From theory to implementation. Identifiability matters here because these interaction patterns are reusable and expected to improve generalizability across different scenarios. These results pin down the inductive bias that our method should approximate: (i) concept encoders rich enough to preserve local binding factors, (ii) sparse interaction support or low-rank interaction subspaces, and (iii) exploitation of the collider-induced dependence under B=1 . ## 3 CAUSAL BIND : Implementation 

We implement the sparse causal principle of § 2. We develop three variants: C AUSAL BIND SP ,CAUSAL BIND LR , and C AUSAL BIND EMB . Due to space, we focus on C AUSAL BIND SP , the sparse concept-pair implementation; supplements for C AUSAL BIND SP , C AUSAL BIND LR , and C AUSAL -BIND EMB are given in App. A5.2, App. A5.3, and App. A5.4. ## 4 Experiments 

Experimental setup. We evaluate on two complementary benchmarks: DUD-E [Mysinger et al., 2012], which covers broad target diversity, and LIT-PCBA [Tran-Nguyen et al., 2020, Mayr et al., 2018], which suppresses property-based shortcuts and is the stricter test of genuine binding dis-crimination. We report standard metrics: AUROC, BEDROC 80 .5, and EF@1%, with the latter two emphasizing early enrichment. We adopt the same curated assay-level training dataset as LigU-nity [Feng et al., 2025] and HypSeek [Wang et al., 2026], constructed from ChEMBL [Mendez et al., 2019], BindingDB [Gilson et al., 2016], and PDBBind [Liu et al., 2017]. As in these baselines, we remove every ChEMBL/BindingDB assay whose target UniProt ID appears in DUD-E, LIT-PCBA, or DEKOIS 2.0, reducing this portion from 26,729 to 18,316 assays, while the PDBBind pairs are not filtered by UniProt ID (App. A5). The evaluation is therefore a cross-dataset generalization protocol rather than a random split of the training pool, with about 476K training molecules and 130K pockets. All models are trained for 50 epochs on four NVIDIA A100 GPUs under the same pipeline for a fair comparison, and the main tables report evaluation results from the last checkpoint, which avoids tuning checkpoint selection to the held-out test benchmarks in the cross-dataset generalization setting. 

Main Comparison on Virtual Screening. Table 1 compares C AUSAL BIND with three families of virtual-screening methods: classical docking systems, structure-aware scoring models on protein-ligand complexes, and retrieval-based representation learners. Retrieval methods are strongest in this large-scale regime, so the key comparison is against recent retrieval baselines. C AUSAL BIND SP 

improves over LigUnity and HypSeek on all six metrics, with the largest gains on LIT-PCBA early enrichment, where property shortcuts are least useful: EF@1% rises from 6.50 to 8.56 over LigUnity and from 4.62 to 8.56 over HypSeek, while BEDROC 80 .5 improves from 0.075 and 0.061 to 0.097 .Against the paper-reported results of the concurrent S 2Drug [He et al., 2026], which uses a heavier two-stage sequence–structure pipeline, S 2Drug attains the best DUD-E BEDROC 80 .5, while all three CAUSAL BIND variants achieve higher DUD-E AUROC and EF@1% and higher values on all three LIT-PCBA metrics; a matched EF@0.5% comparison is given in App. A6.2. C AUSAL BIND LR and CAUSAL BIND EMB further show that the same constrained-interaction principle remains strong under explicit low-rank and embedding-level implementations. We also report C AUSAL BIND ATOM , an atom-attentive version of C AUSAL BIND SP with the same configuration, whose concept extractor attends over the full per-atom token sequences of the Uni-Mol molecule and pocket encoders rather than their pooled tokens, so that concepts can be traced to individual atoms (App. A6.4). It attains the best DUD-E AUROC and EF@1%, but weaker LIT-PCBA early enrichment than the other variants. Ablation Diagnostics. Table 2 reports both component ablations and implementation diagnostics. For C AUSAL BIND SP , removing Lspa weakens early enrichment, suggesting that the concept-pair mask is most useful when regularized toward a simple interaction structure. For C AUSAL BIND LR ,the rank-one factorization is retained in both rows; adding Lspa has limited effect, so the gain should be attributed to the explicit low-rank parameterization rather than to sparsity regularization. For CAUSAL BIND EMB , imposing a rank-one pooled embedding mask sharply degrades performance, showing that rank compression must act directly on the concept axis rather than after pooling. 

Hyperparameter Study. We sweep K (concept count), dc (concept dimension), λ (sparsity weight λspa ), and L (Perceiver depth). Figure 4 and Table 3 show robustness around our setting (K=128 , d c=1024 , λ =10 −4, L =4 ), with failures: K=256 over-parameterizes, dc ≤ 256 underfits, and λ ≥ 10 −2 collapses both benchmarks. Within the useful regime, λ=10 −4 favors LIT-PCBA and 

λ=10 −3 favors DUD-E, suggesting that the sparsity level is benchmark-dependent but moderate. Overall, the model benefits from a soft interaction bottleneck, while hard-zero masking does not. The antichain statistics in Table 3 connect these regimes to the theory: the default λ=10 −4 has no hard zeros, so it violates the antichain clause of Condition 2.i and is instead governed by the low-rank regime of Condition 3.i (Theorem 3); λ=10 −3 yields an exact sparse-antichain support with zero violations, matching Condition 2.i (Theorem 2); and λ=10 −2 preserves the antichain property but over-sparsifies the interaction, so the antichain property alone is insufficient for retrieval performance. 

Out-of-distribution generalization and interpretability. We further evaluate zero-shot transfer on DEKOIS 2.0 [Bauer et al., 2013] under target-level and joint target–scaffold out-of-distribution splits, where C AUSAL BIND improves over HypSeek and LigUnity on AUROC and BEDROC 80 .5 in both settings (App. A6.3). An interpretability case study on the Thyroid Hormone Receptor β (THRB) complex from DUD-E further shows that observed binding contacts concentrate within a learned concept pair, and that masking the associated atoms reduces the predicted binding score far more than matched random masks (App. A6.4).

## 5 Discussions and Conclusion 

Limitations, Broader Impact, and Reproducibility. Our evidence is centered primarily on retrieval-based virtual screening under cross-dataset generalization, especially the two main benchmarks, DUD-E and LIT-PCBA, with an additional Merck-FEP-8 affinity-ranking transfer analysis in App. A6.1.9 and target- and scaffold-level out-of-distribution evaluation on DEKOIS 2.0 in App. A6.3. This is the right, deliberately focused scope for the main question of the paper, but it does not yet establish uniform gains across sequence-family or time-stratified splits or larger benchmark collections, and our interpretability analysis provides model-level attribution rather than proof of a physical causal mechanism (see App. A7). As with any virtual-screening method, our model could be misused to prioritize ligands for harmful targets. Although it is a scoring tool rather than a generator, downstream deployment should still be paired with safety and experimental validation filters before any wet-lab follow-up. All source code is publicly available in our GitHub repository to support reproducibility. 

Conclusion. This paper revisits retrieval-based virtual screening from a structural perspective. Existing methods largely rely on dense shared-space alignment, although protein-molecule binding is typically driven by a small set of local compatibilities. We formalized this mismatch with a V-structure causal model under binder-only selection, showing that unconstrained dense interaction is non-identifiable, while sparse interaction can yield component-wise identifiability and low-rank interaction can yield subspace identifiability. C AUSAL BIND turns this principle into a concept-based retrieval framework with three structure-constrained implementations, and consistently improves over strong baselines on DUD-E and LIT-PCBA, with especially clear gains on LIT-PCBA early enrichment. Beyond the two main benchmarks, the learned interaction structures transfer to target-and scaffold-level out-of-distribution splits of DEKOIS 2.0, and the learned masks exhibit the sparse-antichain and low-rank regimes predicted by the theory. Overall, scalable virtual screening should learn not only better shared embeddings but also more reusable cross-modal interaction structures.

## A1 Drug Discovery Pipeline and Method Positioning 

Figure A1 places C AUSAL BIND within the broader drug-discovery workflow. The key point, consis-tent with the main text, is that our method is designed for the high-throughput virtual-screening stage :the part of the pipeline that must rapidly down-select a very large candidate library before more ex-pensive docking and experimental follow-up. In other words, C AUSAL BIND is not a pose-prediction or lead-optimization method; it is a retrieval-style early filter whose job is to improve the quality of the top-ranked compounds passed to later stages.

The next stage is virtual screening , where methods rank compounds by predicted binding relevance and reduce the candidate set to something tractable for follow-up; this is the stage where C AUSAL BIND operates. More expensive docking or rescoring is then applied to a much smaller set, typically 10 2–10 3 compounds, using physics-based or structure-aware scoring functions to reason in greater detail about poses and local interactions. Only after this reduction do compounds proceed to experimental validation , usually tens to hundreds of biochemical or cell-based assays, followed by hit-to-lead optimization, and eventually the even narrower stages of clinical testing and approval . The practical role of C AUSAL BIND is therefore upstream: it improves which candidates are allowed to reach these far more expensive downstream stages. 

Why virtual screening matters. The virtual-screening stage is where the paper’s evaluation metrics become practically meaningful. This stage is responsible for the largest candidate reduction before wet-lab testing, so the quality of the very top of the ranking matters far more than average prediction quality over a fixed benchmark. This is exactly why the main text emphasizes early-enrichment metrics such as BEDROC 80 .5 and EF@1%: even a modest improvement there can substantially reduce the number of false positives advanced to docking or assay validation. Our focus on sparse binding determinants is intended to improve this high-value early-filtering regime. 

Complementary to docking. This positioning also clarifies why the paper compares docking, structure-aware scoring, and retrieval as distinct regimes rather than as direct substitutes. Docking remains valuable because it reasons explicitly about poses and local geometry, but it is too expensive to serve as the first-pass search over very large libraries. Retrieval-style methods occupy a different 17 operating point: they trade mechanistic detail for scale and are therefore best used upstream of docking. C AUSAL BIND fits this role. Its contribution is to improve the representation quality of that pre-filtering stage so that the compounds passed downstream are more likely to contain true binders. 

## A2 Extended Related Work 

From docking to scalable screening. Classical virtual screening is dominated by docking pipelines such as Glide, AutoDock Vina, and Surflex, which explicitly search binding poses and rank com-pounds using physics-inspired scoring functions [Friesner et al., 2004, Trott and Olson, 2010, Spitzer and Jain, 2012]. Their strengths are interpretability and direct geometric reasoning about binding modes, but their cost grows quickly with library size and target flexibility. In practical workflows this often makes docking a downstream stage, applied after an initial coarse filter rather than as the first-pass search over millions of compounds. This is exactly the regime targeted in the main text: large-scale pre-filtering, where retrieval speed and early enrichment matter more than full pose resolution. 

Learned scoring functions and pose-aware models. A large literature replaces hand-engineered docking scores with learned interaction models over protein-molecule complexes. Gnina introduced deep learning into docking-time scoring with 3D CNNs [McNutt et al., 2021]; RTMScore, GenScore, EquiScore, PIGNet, and PLANET further strengthened this line with graph, transformer, equivariant, and physics-informed architectures [Shen et al., 2022, 2023, Cao et al., 2024, Moon et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2023]. Related structure-based models such as TankBind and BigBind learn from geometric complexes or large-scale structural supervision to improve affinity prediction and screening robustness [Lu et al., 2022, Brocidiacono et al., 2023]. These methods are highly relevant baselines because they capture detailed intermolecular geometry, but they usually assume a candidate complex, pose, or structure-conditioned interaction graph at inference time. By contrast, our focus is on 

retrieval-style virtual screening where compound scoring must remain fast enough for large libraries. 

Affinity prediction beyond screening. Drug–target affinity (DTA) models such as DeepDTA and GraphDTA predict binding strength from sequence or graph representations without necessarily producing an efficient retrieval system [Öztürk et al., 2018, Nguyen et al., 2021]. This line clarified how learned molecular and protein embeddings can support affinity estimation, but it is typically evaluated on regression-style benchmarks rather than target-wise early enrichment. The distinction matters for our paper because the objective is not simply to estimate affinity well on average; it is to identify which compounds should be placed at the very top of the screening list. 

Retrieval-based contrastive learning for virtual screening. The recent shift most relevant to our work is the move from explicit complex scoring to shared embedding spaces . DrugCLIP [Gao et al., 2023] framed protein-molecule virtual screening as dense retrieval, using Uni-Mol-based encoders and CLIP-style contrastive learning [Radford et al., 2021, Zhou et al., 2023]. This design substantially improved scalability and helped establish retrieval-based virtual screening as a viable alternative to docking, a direction later amplified by a genome-scale Science study built on DrugCLIP-style contrastive screening [Jia et al., 2026]. Subsequent methods enriched this paradigm in different ways: DrugHash compresses embeddings into binary codes for memory-efficient screening [Han et al., 2025]; LigUnity unifies virtual screening with hit-to-lead affinity ranking through scaffold discrimination and pharmacophore ranking in a shared pocket–ligand space [Feng et al., 2025]; HypSeek argues that hyperbolic geometry better captures hierarchical and fine-grained affinity variation than Euclidean retrieval alone [Wang et al., 2026]; and the concurrent S 2Drug bridges protein sequence and 3D structure through ChEMBL sequence pretraining and PDBBind fine-tuning with residue-level sequence–structure fusion and an auxiliary binding-site task [He et al., 2026]. These papers collectively show that the field has moved from asking whether retrieval can work at all to asking what kind of structural bias should govern the shared space. The present paper answers that question by shifting the focus from dense holistic alignment to sparse or low-rank cross-modal interaction. 

Benchmark bias and generalization. A recurring concern in virtual screening is that benchmark artifacts can inflate apparent performance. Hidden biases in DUD-E and related datasets have long been known to create shortcuts based on simple physicochemical cues rather than genuine binding recognition [Chen et al., 2019]. More recent analyses argue that debiasing is essential for robust 18 binding-affinity generalization across targets and chemical series [Graber et al., 2025]. This motivates the paper’s emphasis on LIT-PCBA and on early-enrichment gains under more realistic decoys: the goal is not merely to improve in-distribution ranking, but to learn representations that remain useful when easy shortcuts disappear. 

Why three regimes? Core computational pattern. Although all three regimes ultimately rank protein-molecule pairs, their inference-time computational pattern differs sharply, and this is what determines their practical scaling behaviour and where their inductive bias has to live. The defining feature of retrieval-based methods is that protein and molecule embeddings are computed indepen-dently (a dual-tower architecture); the binding score then reduces to a similarity, typically a dot product, between two pre-computable vectors, exactly as in dense passage retrieval in NLP [Oord et al., 2018, Radford et al., 2021] or image–text retrieval. This pattern is what unlocks very large-scale screening, but it also constrains where cross-modal interaction can be expressed: the interaction has to be encoded into the representation or the similarity function, rather than being computed by a joint pair-input network at inference time. This is precisely where C AUSAL BIND intervenes. Instead of leaving the retrieval score as a dense holistic similarity, we impose sparse concept-level interaction inside the retrieval regime itself. Among structure-aware learning-based methods, Gnina, RTMScore, GenScore, EquiScore, TankBind, and BigBind all retain a joint pair-input view of the protein-molecule problem. Gnina and RTMScore are closely tied to docked-pose scoring, inheriting much of the pose dependence of classical docking. GenScore and EquiScore strengthen this line with more expressive learned complex representations, while TankBind pushes further toward end-to-end geometric reasoning over binding sites and com-plexes. BigBind is especially notable for highlighting the importance of large-scale curated structural supervision. These methods are relevant because they capture explicit intermolecular geometry, but their inference-time cost and pair-conditioned architecture make them fundamentally different from the large-scale retrieval regime studied in our main experiments. The retrieval baselines are the closest comparators. DrugCLIP established the modern dense-retrieval formulation for virtual screening and demonstrated that CLIP-style contrastive learning with Uni-Mol encoders can scale to very large libraries. DrugHash preserves that same dense-alignment 20 philosophy while prioritizing storage and retrieval efficiency through binary hashing, at the cost of some predictive fidelity. LigUnity extends dense shared-space retrieval by coupling virtual screening with hit-to-lead ranking signals, which helps its early-enrichment behavior but does not change the fact that the interaction remains holistic in the shared space. HypSeek is the strongest baseline in this family: it enriches retrieval with hyperbolic geometry, cone-hierarchy regularization, and a protein-sequence branch, yielding a powerful geometric inductive bias but still no explicit structural prior on which cross-modal interactions should matter. This is the point at which C AUSAL BIND differs. We stay in the retrieval regime and keep the scalability advantages of independently encoded molecule and protein representations, but we change the structural bottleneck through which binding information must pass. Compared with docking or pose-aware scoring, C AUSAL BIND does not attempt to replace explicit structural modeling; instead it improves the front-end screening stage that decides which compounds deserve expensive downstream analysis. Compared with DrugCLIP, DrugHash, LigUnity, and HypSeek, we leave the high-level retrieval pipeline intact but modify the representation bottleneck: a shared ConceptExtractor decom-poses each modality into atomic concepts, and a sparse cross-modal mask highlights which concept pairs are causally relevant for binding. Semantically, prior retrieval methods ask what inductive bias should structure the embedding space and answer with metric geometry, compression, or training-time objectives. C AUSAL BIND instead asks what inductive bias should structure the interaction between 

molecule and protein representations, and answers with a sparse or low-rank bipartite kernel grounded in the V-structure causal model of Section 2. In that sense, C AUSAL BIND is best understood not as a new backbone family, but as an orthogonal structural prior inside retrieval-based virtual screening itself. 

Causal representation learning. The conceptual basis for our method comes from causal repre-sentation learning [Schölkopf et al., 2021, Hyvarinen et al., 2019], especially work showing that multimodal observations and sparse graphical structure can improve identifiability of latent vari-ables [Daunhawer et al., 2023, Kivva et al., 2021]. ConceptAligner [Xie et al., 2025] operationalized this intuition in a multimodal generative setting, while Sun et al. [2025] extended related ideas to biomedical multimodal data with identifiability guarantees for the latent causal variables. We adapt this literature to protein-molecule modeling under a different causal graph: unlike text-to-image gen-eration, neither modality generates the other; instead molecule and protein concepts jointly determine a binding event through a selected collider. This shift from a directional chain to a V-structure is what motivates the sparse bipartite mask at the center of C AUSAL BIND .

Causal discovery and selection bias. Complementary to causal representation learning, causal discovery recovers causal graphs among observed variables, with recent advances that handle deter-ministic relations [Li et al., 2024a], decentralized heterogeneous data [Li et al., 2024b], and scalable DAG structure learning [Ziu et al., 2024]. A related line of work studies how selection distorts the observed distribution: causal graphs with latent confounders and post-treatment selection can be characterized and learned from interventional data [Luo et al., 2026b], gene regulatory networks can be inferred in the presence of selection bias [Luo et al., 2026a], and selective mechanisms have also been exploited in text generation [Tang et al., 2025]. Our setting shares this concern with selection: training data contain only observed binding pairs, and C AUSAL BIND explicitly models this binder-only selection as conditioning on a collider when deriving which interaction structures remain identifiable. 

## A3 Biological Observations of Sparse Binding 

Hot spots and sparse binding energy. A direct biological motivation for our sparse-interaction view comes from the classical hot-spot literature on molecular recognition. In the human growth hormone–receptor system, Clackson and Wells [1995] combined the crystal structure of the complex with alanine-scanning mutagenesis and found that many side chains participate in the physical interface, but the energetic contribution is highly concentrated: a central hydrophobic region on the receptor side, dominated by two tryptophan residues, accounts for more than three quarters of the binding free energy. This result separates the structural epitope , namely the broad set of contacts visible in the complex, from the functional epitope , namely the much smaller subset of contacts that actually maintains affinity. Bogan and Thorn [1998] then showed that this concentration of binding energy is a general property of protein interfaces rather than an isolated example: hot spots 21 are formed by a small subset of residues, are enriched in tryptophan, tyrosine, and arginine, and are often surrounded by less energetic residues that shield the hot spot from bulk solvent. These findings support the central modeling assumption of C AUSAL BIND : the binding signal should not be represented as a uniformly dense agreement between whole protein and molecule embeddings. Instead, it is more natural to model binding through a limited number of cross-modal compatibilities, while treating much of the remaining surface or molecular context as supportive, redundant, or potentially nuisance-correlated. 

Pharmacophores as invariant binding determinants. Even though induced fit allows confor-mational adjustment, medicinal chemistry has long emphasized that certain feature types remain consistently binding-relevant across conformational states. These are the pharmacophoric fea-tures [Wermuth, 2006]: hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, hydrophobic centers, aromatic groups, charged moieties, and related steric/electronic motifs that repeatedly determine whether a ligand can engage a target pocket productively. The exact geometry of a bound complex may shift, but these feature types remain the stable determinants that make those local interactions possible. In the language of this paper, they are the biologically plausible candidates for the “atomic concepts” that should survive across proteins, ligands, and conformational variants. 

Why static structures are still informative. A natural concern is that if induced fit is dynamic, then learning from static structures might miss the relevant mechanism. The key point is that our goal is not to recover every conformational degree of freedom; it is to recover the interaction-relevant determinants that persist across such variability. A hydroxyl group remains a hydrogen-bond donor; an aromatic ring remains capable of π-stacking; a hydrophobic patch remains nonpolar even if side chains rotate slightly around it. Benchmarks such as DUD-E and LIT-PCBA are built from static structures, yet successful virtual-screening methods already rely on the fact that these chemically meaningful determinants are more stable than the incidental conformational details. Our method does not remove this assumption; it makes it more explicit by organizing the representation around sparse cross-modal interactions rather than around dense global similarity. 

From biology to the modeling choice in C AUSAL BIND . This biological picture motivates the two central methodological choices in the paper. First, if binding depends on a small set of recurring local determinants, then each modality should be decomposed into smaller concept-like units rather than treated as a single holistic vector. Second, if only a subset of those units actually participates in binding for a given target family, then the cross-modal interaction should itself be sparse or low-rank rather than dense. This is exactly what the ConceptExtractor and SparseMask are designed to implement. The model is therefore not claiming that every learned concept is a textbook pharmacophore; rather, it is imposing an inductive bias that is much closer to the biological mechanism than dense shared-space alignment. 

Relevance to generalization and drug discovery. This interpretation also clarifies why the largest gains tend to appear on the more bias-controlled LIT-PCBA benchmark. If a method relies on shortcut correlations, it may rank compounds well on easier datasets without truly isolating binding determinants. By contrast, a representation organized around invariant local compatibilities should transfer better to novel targets and novel scaffolds, because those compatibilities are closer to the actual mechanism medicinal chemists reason about during lead optimization. In this sense, the biological motivation of C AUSAL BIND is not only explanatory but also practical: it supports the paper’s broader claim that sparse concept-level interaction is a more plausible route to robust virtual screening than dense holistic alignment. 

## A4 Theoretical Framework: Selection and Identifiability 

This appendix provides the complete theoretical framework for C AUSAL BIND . We cast protein-molecule binding prediction as a Heckman-style selection problem on a V-structure causal graph: the training set consists only of binding pairs ( B = 1 ), and our goal is to identify the latent concepts 

cM , cP and the bipartite interaction mask M from this selection-biased observation. Following the didactic organization of SEDGE [Zhang et al., 2026] and ConceptAligner [Xie et al., 2025], we proceed from a careful SCM formulation (A4.1) through a worked KM = KP = 2 toy case (A4.3) to the general non-identifiability and identifiability results (A4.4–A4.5), a low-rank subspace-identifiability result (A4.6), and an explicit comparison to SEDGE (A4.7). Each theorem subsection 22 is organized into a proof sketch, an intuitive example, and a full proof, and a later discussion in the experimental appendix (A6.8) returns to how these identifiability results should be interpreted in light of the learned masks. Our framework borrows the Heckman-selection machinery recently formalized for vision–language concept generation by Xie et al. [2025] and for compositional extrapolation by SEDGE, but differs in four crucial ways: (i) we have a collider causal structure (V-structure) rather than a chain; (ii) we have two symmetric observation sources (molecule and protein) rather than one feature vector; (iii) our binding kernel is bilinear-sparse rather than a parametric MLP head; and (iv) we replace the SEDGE-style “conservative” assumption ( P (S=0) ≈ 0) with a low-rank binding kernel assumption that is better suited to drug discovery, where most protein-molecule pairs do not bind.

A4.2 Discussion of Assumptions 

The formal assumptions and theorem-specific conditions are stated in the main text, so we do not repeat them here. Instead, this subsection explains what each assumption or condition contributes to the proofs and how strong it should be read. We keep the main-text notation throughout: Assump-tions 1–3 are shared by all theorems, Conditions 2.i–2.ii belong to Theorem 2, and Conditions 3.i–3.ii belong to Theorem 3. • Assumption 1 (concept recoverability). This assumption says that the concepts entering the binding mechanism are statistically accessible from the observed molecule or protein. It should not be read as requiring a global inverse for the full observation maps gM , g P or for the neural encoders used in implementation. The proofs use it only to pull the binder-selected observation distribution pD (xM , x P ) back to concept coordinates on the data support. • Assumption 2 (prior independence). This is the V-structure assumption: before conditioning on binding, molecule-side and protein-side concepts are independent objects. The observed dependence in pD then comes from conditioning on the collider B = 1 . Without this assumption, cross-modal dependence could already be present before selection, and the proof could not attribute it to the binding mechanism. • Assumption 3 (positivity / coverage). This is the minimal support requirement. If a concept never appears in binder-selected data, it leaves no statistical trace and cannot be identified by any method. The assumption does not require uniform coverage of all concept combinations; it only rules out concepts that are completely absent from the selected support. • Condition 2.i (sparse antichain interaction). Sparsity alone is not enough for component-wise identifiability: two concepts can still be confounded if one concept’s interaction support is absorbed by another’s. The antichain requirement gives each molecule-side and protein-side concept a distinct combinatorial footprint in the bipartite interaction graph. This is the structural condition that turns the collider-induced dependence into component-wise recovery. • Condition 2.ii (sufficient variability). This is the finite-probe richness condition for the sparse theorem. The mixed derivatives of ηD (cP ; cM ) = log pD (cP | cM ) are the statistical objects that survive after marginal terms are removed in the proof. Requiring the response vectors at finitely many molecule-side probes to be linearly independent rules out degenerate selected distributions in which different interaction patterns induce the same curvature. 24 • Condition 3.i (low-rank interaction kernel). This is the structural condition for the subspace theorem. It replaces exact concept-pair support recovery with the assumption that the binding score has a minimal rank-r separable factorization. Thus the target of identification changes: we no longer seek a unique entry-wise mask, but the molecule-side and protein-side subspaces spanned by the low-rank interaction functions. • Condition 3.ii (low-rank sufficient variability). This is the statistical richness condition for the subspace theorem, not another low-rank constraint. It requires the mode-evaluation matrices to have full rank at finitely many molecule-side and protein-side probes. This guarantees that the low-rank modes specified by Condition 3.i are sufficiently excited on the selected support, so their spans can be recovered from the observed distribution.

A4.5 Theorem 2: Component-Wise Identifiability Under Sparse Interaction 

We now state the central identifiability result. Our proof strategy adapts the component-wise identi-fication arguments of Kivva et al. [2021] and Xie et al. [2025] to the V-structure setting, using the collider-induced dependence from Assumption 2 plus conditioning on B = 1 as the information source and the sparse antichain structure in Condition 2.i as the disentangling constraint. 

Theorem 2 (Component-wise identifiability under sparse interaction) . Suppose Assumptions 1–3 and Conditions 2.i–2.ii of Theorem 2 hold, and let pD (xM , x P ) = p(xM , x P | B = 1) be the observed selection-biased distribution. Then, within the stated sparse-antichain model class, the latent concepts (cM , cP ) and the bipartite support of M are component-wise identifiable from 

pD in the sense of Definition 1. 

Discussion. This is the appendix counterpart of Theorem 2 and the main positive result of the paper. Condition 2.i provides the structural separation that sparse recovery needs: without an antichain support pattern, one concept can still be absorbed into another. Condition 2.ii provides the complementary finite-probe richness condition: the mixed-derivative response vectors vr (cP )

induced by finitely many molecule-side concept values must be linearly independent. These vectors are exactly the statistical objects used below to separate distinct interaction patterns after the marginal terms have been removed by cross differentiation. The result is a conditional sufficiency statement for the sparse-antichain class: generic dense GL coordinate changes are not admissible competitors because they usually destroy this support structure. The theorem therefore isolates the exact point at which collider-induced dependence becomes informative rather than merely confounded.

A4.6 Theorem 3: Subspace Identifiability Under Low-Rank Interaction 

Condition 2.i requires that some sparse mask exist; it does not specify its structure. For protein-molecule binding, we argue that the subspace-identifiability alternative in Condition 3.i is also natural empirically: the binding kernel has low rank. 

Motivation. Protein–molecule binding in drug discovery is dominated by a small number of pharmacophoric motifs: hydrogen bonds, π-stacking, hydrophobic packing, and electrostatic comple-mentarity. It is therefore physically reasonable that the complete binding response can be decomposed into r independent interaction modes , each characterized by a pair (uk, v k) describing how each modality contributes to mode k. We expect r to be small; typical estimates from medicinal chemistry place r at 5–20 for a given target family. 

Why this replaces SEDGE’s conservative assumption. SEDGE’s identifiability argument relies on 

P (S=0) ≈ 0, i.e., “unseen configurations are rare.” This is false for binding: most protein-molecule pairs do not bind, so P (B=0) ≫ P (B=1) . Condition 3.i replaces this with a physics-motivated structural constraint on the kernel itself, preserving identifiability without requiring the pathological SEDGE-style coverage condition. 

Architectural implication. A4.7 Summary and Discussion of the Theory 

The results above establish a coherent theoretical picture for C AUSAL BIND . First, the structural causal model clarifies why binder-only training should be treated as a Heckman-style selection problem on a V-structure rather than as ordinary supervised learning. Second, Theorem 1 shows that dense holistic interaction is fundamentally non-identifiable under this selection mechanism. Third, Theorem 2 shows that sparse interaction can restore identifiability up to the standard ambiguities of permutation, component-wise reparameterization, and joint rescaling. Finally, Theorem 3 gives a subspace-identifiability result when exact sparse support is too strong: even if entry-wise mask identification fails, the dominant interaction subspace can still remain identifiable. 

What the three results establish together. The theoretical development above should be read as a progression from impossibility, to component-wise identifiability under strong sparse structure, to subspace identifiability under weaker but more realistic low-rank structure. Theorem 1 shows that without additional constraints, binder-only observations do not determine a unique latent interaction mechanism: many dense factorizations can explain the same selected distribution. Theorem 2 then shows that component-wise identifiability becomes possible when the interaction mask is sparse in a combinatorially distinctive way and the selected conditional family varies sufficiently across molecule-side concepts. Finally, Theorem 3 shows that even when exact element-wise sparsity is relaxed, the dominant interaction modes remain identifiable at the subspace level under a low-rank kernel assumption. 

Why the causal structure matters. The key source of information is the V-structure 

cM → B ← cP ,

not an externally supplied domain index or an auxiliary supervision signal. Before conditioning on binding, the molecule-side and protein-side concepts are independent under Assumption 2; after restricting to B=1 , they become statistically coupled through the collider. The identifiability problem is therefore not solved by adding a second dataset or a separate view, but by exploiting the dependence structure already induced by binder-only selection. The sparse-support theorem uses that dependence to establish component-wise identifiability up to standard equivariances, while the low-rank theorem uses it to establish subspace identifiability of the leading interaction modes when exact support identification is too strong a requirement.

A5.1 Relation Between the Two-Modal Theory and the Three-Modal Implementation 

The theory in Sections 2 and A4 is deliberately written for the simplest protein-molecule setting with two modalities: one molecule view and one protein view. The implementation, however, uses three modalities: molecule structure, pocket structure, and protein sequence. This does not contradict the theory; rather, it instantiates the same two-modal idea twice. Concretely, the model contains two retrieval branches, molecule-pocket and molecule-sequence, with score matrices Upoc ,mol and 

Useq ,mol . Each branch is a protein-molecule pairing of exactly the kind analyzed in the theory, and each branch is equipped with its own constrained interaction bottleneck. A5.2.2 Concrete Parameter Assignments and Experimental Setting 

We now turn to the code-level side of reproducibility for C AUSAL BIND SP : what values are assigned to the key hyperparameters, how the inherited loss weights are instantiated in code, and what training/evaluation settings are shared across the reported runs. 

Training setup and optimization. We build on the HypSeek code base [Wang et al., 2026] with pretrained Uni-Mol encoders for molecules and pockets and an ESM-2 encoder for protein sequence. Training follows the same three-branch retrieval pipeline on the blended ChEMBL+BindingDB 

corpus ( 18 ,316 assays after removing assays that overlap the FEP benchmarks and every assay whose target UniProt ID appears in DUD-E, LIT-PCBA, or DEKOIS 2.0; as in the LigUnity and HypSeek protocol, the PDBBind structural pairs are not filtered by UniProt ID, so some PDBBind complexes share targets with these benchmarks), with CASF used for validation when validation-selected protocols are needed. Unless noted otherwise, all reported runs train for 50 epochs on 

4×A100-80GB GPUs with per-GPU batch size 24 (distributed world size 4, effective global batch size 96 ), Adam optimizer, learning rate 10 −4, polynomial decay, warmup ratio 0.06 , weight decay 

0, gradient clipping at 1.0, and fp16 mixed precision. In our cluster logs and planning estimates, a typical 50-epoch run takes several hours on 4×A100, so full sweeps scale roughly linearly with the number of variants. The default seed is 1; additional seeds are only introduced in the dedicated robustness checks. All compared retrieval runs (HypSeek, concept-level ablations, concept-level sweeps, and embedding-level variants) share this optimization setup unless a subsection explicitly states otherwise. 

Inference and storage. CAUSAL BIND remains a retrieval-style dual-tower model: molecule-side and protein-side concept features are computed independently, and the fixed branch masks are applied before producing score matrices. Thus library molecules can still be precomputed rather than passed through a joint protein-molecule network for every target. The explicit K × K CAUSAL BIND SP 

masks add a small fixed branch computation, while C AUSAL BIND LR reduces mask parameters from K2 to 2Kr per branch. For billion-scale deployment one would typically combine these representations with approximate-nearest-neighbor indexing or distillation; the present experiments evaluate ranking quality rather than an optimized production index. 

Loss weights and the relation between paper notation and code notation. In Section 3, we write the training objective using a compact notation with a sparsity weight λspa and a grouped geometric term λreg Lreg . In the code, however, the inherited HypSeek regularization is implemented through 35 several explicit coefficients rather than through a single scalar λreg . Concretely, the cone-hierarchy term uses γchl = 0 .1 together with λrad = 0 .5 and λang = 0 .5, while the additional regularization bundle uses λangu = 0 .1 and λhet = 0 .1. Thus, the paper’s Lreg should be read as a compact umbrella notation for the inherited angular and heterogeneity regularizers, while λreg is not a single independently tuned scalar in the current implementation. The only new coefficient introduced by CAUSAL BIND itself is λspa , whose values are reported explicitly in the corresponding experiment tables. 

Evaluation protocol. We evaluate on DUD-E (102 targets) and LIT-PCBA (15 targets). For each target we rank all molecules in the test set and compute AUROC, BEDROC ( α = 80 .5), and EF@1%. Per-target metrics are macro-averaged across targets. 

Evaluation metrics. AUROC measures overall ranking quality across the full ranked list: it is the probability that a randomly chosen active is ranked above a randomly chosen inactive, so it reflects global discrimination rather than early retrieval. BEDROC 80 .5 is an early-enrichment metric that exponentially upweights the top of the ranked list; the parameter α = 80 .5 is the standard virtual-screening setting that places roughly 80% of the weight within the top ∼ 2% of ranks, making the metric highly sensitive to whether true actives appear near the front. EF@1% (enrichment factor at the top 1% ) compares the number of actives retrieved in the top 1% of the ranked list against the number expected from random ranking. In practical screening, BEDROC 80 .5 and EF@1% are often more informative than AUROC because only a small top-ranked subset is typically advanced to experimental follow-up.

A6.1 CAUSAL BIND SP : Sparse Concept-Pair Results A6.1.1 Extended Analyses for the Main Experiments Why does causal concept extraction help? The main paper argues that dense holistic alignment is too coarse a structural prior for protein-molecule binding, because binding is typically driven by a small number of local compatibilities rather than by uniform all-to-all agreement. The role of causal concept extraction is to operationalize this inductive bias. The concept extractor encourages each modality to decompose into a smaller set of binding-relevant factors, and the sparse interaction mask then decides which cross-modal pairs matter for prediction. In the scratch-training setting, this provides a strong bias toward disentangled structure. In the pretrained setting considered in our main experiments, it plays a more selective role: rather than learning chemistry and protein semantics from nothing, the module filters a pretrained representation and suppresses features that are predictive but not genuinely binding-relevant. 

Why do the gains concentrate on early enrichment? Across both experimental settings, the improvements in BEDROC 80 .5 and EF@1% are often larger than the improvements in AUROC. This pattern is important rather than incidental. AUROC measures global ranking quality over the entire candidate list, whereas BEDROC 80 .5 and EF@1% emphasize whether true actives are pushed into the very top of the ranked list, which is the practically important regime in virtual screening. Our interpretation is that the sparse interaction prior is especially helpful when the model must decide which few candidates deserve the highest confidence, rather than when it is merely asked to produce a smooth global ordering. 

Why is LIT-PCBA the stricter test? The larger improvements on LIT-PCBA than on DUD-E suggest that the causal prior is most useful when shortcut features become unreliable. DUD-E remains a useful benchmark because of its scale and broad target coverage, but LIT-PCBA is deliberately constructed to reduce property-based biases and therefore makes it harder for a model to succeed by exploiting easy decoy artifacts. When improvements persist on LIT-PCBA, especially in early-enrichment metrics, they are more plausibly reflecting better binding discrimination rather than better exploitation of dataset quirks. This is why the appendix repeatedly treats LIT-PCBA as the main stress test for whether the sparse interaction prior is doing the intended job.

A6.1.8 ROC-Enrichment (RE) on DUD-E 

ROC-enrichment (RE) is another early-recognition metric, complementary to BEDROC 80 .5 and EF@1%. At a fixed false-positive-rate threshold α, it is defined as RE@ α = TPR( α)/α , so larger values mean that the model retrieves actives much more efficiently than random ranking in the very early part of the list. Following prior reporting practice [Wang et al., 2026], we report RE at 0.5% ,

1% , 2% , and 5% false-positive-rate thresholds on DUD-E. We report RE only on DUD-E because it is the more standard setting in prior work and provides a more stable target-averaged estimate than LIT-PCBA. A6.6.3 Theory Correspondence 

The theory correspondence of the embedding-level variant is simpler, but also narrower, than for the main concept-level model. Because the mask acts on pooled embeddings rather than on explicit concept pairs, it does not naturally match the sparse-support picture behind Condition 2.i and Theorem 2. In particular, there is no direct analogue of concept-pair support geometry or the antichain-style condition discussed for the concept-level mask. For that reason, this implementation should not be presented as evidence for the sparse identifiability theorem. By contrast, the connection to the low-rank theorem is much more natural. The embedding-level mask is exactly a constrained interaction operator in a dc-dimensional shared space, so asking whether useful solutions are low-rank is directly meaningful here. In that sense, the variant is much closer in spirit to Condition 3.i and Theorem 3: it tests whether a small number of interaction modes in the pooled cross-modal space is already sufficient for strong retrieval. The most important observation is that the main qualitative conclusion does not depend on a single hyperparameter choice, checkpoint protocol, or implementa-tion variant. Across the main C AUSAL BIND SP comparison (Table A4), the explicit low-rank results (Tables A15–A18), and the embedding-level alternative (Tables A19–A21), constrained cross-modal interaction consistently improves over dense holistic alignment on the retrieval metrics emphasized in the main text, especially early enrichment on LIT-PCBA. 

How the three implementations differ. CAUSAL BIND SP is the main paper model: it keeps explicit concept-pair structure and is therefore the cleanest implementation for studying sparse support, mask calibration, and concept-level interpretation. C AUSAL BIND LR is the direct low-rank concept-basis implementation: it matches the main dense-mask model with far fewer mask parameters and shows that rank-one or near-rank-one interaction is often sufficient. C AUSAL BIND EMB is a pooled-space alternative: it can be very strong empirically, especially on LIT-PCBA early enrichment, but its mask acts after concept pooling and therefore has a weaker connection to component-wise concept identifiability. 

What the hyperparameter studies collectively suggest. The C AUSAL BIND SP K/d c/λ/L scans point to a coherent operating regime: moderate concept granularity, adequate channel width, and moderate interaction depth work best, while overly large dense concept-pair masks can become brittle. The C AUSAL BIND LR scans sharpen this lesson by showing that explicit rank control protects against the large-K collapse and that increasing rank beyond a small value rarely helps. The C AUSAL -BIND EMB scans are smoother, but the strongest setting is still a moderate pooled representation (K=64 , dc=256 ). 

How the sweep connects back to theory. Taken together, the three implementations separate form from empirical regime. C AUSAL BIND SP has the sparse concept-pair form of Theorem 2, but its strongest λ=10 −4 setting behaves more like a low-rank solution; at λ=10 −3 it moves closer to sparse support. C AUSAL BIND LR directly realizes the subspace-identifiability form of Theorem 3 and confirms that the learned interaction can be rank one in practice. C AUSAL BIND EMB supports the broader low-rank interaction principle but should be interpreted as a pooled-space variant rather than a component-wise concept-identifiability result. 

A6.8 Discussion on Theory–Practice Gap 

The theoretical results in Section 2 are intentionally sharp: Theorem 2 describes what becomes identifiable under the sparse-support assumptions, while Theorem 3 gives a subspace-identifiability statement under low-rank interaction. The experiments in App. A6 show that these two theorems are both useful for interpretation, but not in the same way. This subsection makes that distinction explicit by connecting the compact mask statistics in Table 3 with the branchwise diagnostics in Table A9. 

Main takeaway. The central lesson is not that the learned interaction becomes exactly element-wise sparse. Rather, the causal prior pushes training toward structurally simpler cross-modal interactions: stronger sparsification moves C AUSAL BIND SP toward the sparse-support picture, while milder sparsification and explicit C AUSAL BIND LR reveal a low-rank regime. Thus low-rank subspace identifiability is not merely a secondary theoretical case, but an important description of the regime that modern neural training naturally reaches. 

What the experiments do support. The experiments support both theorem regimes, but through different implementations and operating points. In Table 3, ‘ L1 avg’ is the average absolute mask entry, %zeros is the fraction of entries thresholded to exact zero, and r@95% /r@99% are effective ranks explaining 95% /99% of squared singular-value energy. For C AUSAL BIND SP , λ=10 −3 yields strong DUD-E retrieval together with meaningful exact sparsity ( 20 .5% zeros in the compact table), matching the sparse-support picture of Theorem 2; the default λ=10 −4 setting remains dense entry-wise ( 0% zeros) but is strongly low-rank in the dominant energy component ( r@95% = 1 ), matching Theorem 3. For λ ≥ 10 −2, the mask enters an excessive hard-zero regime and performance degrades sharply, so sparsity alone is not the desired behavior. In C AUSAL BIND LR , the rank constraint is imposed directly and the learned masks still collapse to effective rank one, which independently 53 supports the low-rank/subspace interpretation. In C AUSAL BIND EMB , the same low-rank tendency appears after pooling, but this evidence is architectural rather than component-wise. 

Why this is not a contradiction. The theorem statements are intentionally sharp, whereas opti-mization can realize more than one useful structural regime. A dense K×K mask with an L1 penalty can behave as sparse support under stronger regularization or as a low-rank operator under milder regularization; an explicit rank-factorized mask enforces the latter by construction. The empirical message is therefore not that one theorem replaces the other, but that the two theorem regimes identify different useful limits of the same causal interaction prior. 

How we position the current paper. For this reason, we position C AUSAL BIND as a causally motivated structural prior whose empirical behavior is consistent with both theoretical identifiability results. The theory explains why dense holistic interaction is the wrong object, why structural constraints are needed at all, and why sparse-support and low-rank regimes are the right two reference points. The experiments then show how these reference points appear in practice: C AUSAL BIND SP 

connects most directly to sparse concept-pair structure, C AUSAL BIND LR directly validates the subspace-identifiability regime, and C AUSAL BIND EMB shows that a weaker pooled-space version of the same principle can also work well. 

## A7 Limitations, Broader Impact, and Usage of LLMs. 

Limitation on scope of the current evidence. This paper should be read primarily as a study of 

retrieval-based virtual screening under cross-dataset generalization , not as a complete evaluation of protein-molecule modeling in every downstream regime. Accordingly, our main evidence primarily comes from two retrieval benchmarks, DUD-E and LIT-PCBA, while App. A6.1.9 provides a supplementary Merck-FEP-8 affinity-ranking transfer check; the strongest claims still concern early enrichment rather than absolute affinity calibration. This scope is deliberate: the central question of the paper is whether replacing dense holistic alignment with sparse or low-rank cross-modal interaction improves the high-throughput screening stage. It also means that broader claims should be made cautiously. The current experiments do not yet establish how uniformly the gain transfers across protein families, scaffold splits, time-stratified settings, or much larger benchmark collections. 

Broader impact and misuse clarification. Better virtual screening can reduce the cost of early discovery by improving which candidates are advanced to docking and experimental follow-up, potentially accelerating the identification of useful therapeutics. At the same time, any method that improves prioritization of protein-molecule interactions could in principle be misused to search for ligands against harmful targets. Our model is less directly dual-use than a generative molecular-design system, because it is a scoring and ranking tool rather than a molecule generator, but this does not remove the need for safeguards. In practical use, its predictions should be treated as one stage in a controlled pipeline and paired with downstream safety, ADMET, toxicity, and experimental validation filters before any wet-lab deployment. 

Usage of LLMs. LLMs were used to assist with writing and editing, but they are not the source of any scientific claims or empirical results in this paper. All technical content is derived from our own theoretical analysis and experimental work, and all key numbers are directly traceable to our codebase and training logs. We did not use LLMs to generate scientific hypotheses. Their role was limited to language polishing and formatting assistance, which we carefully reviewed and edited for accuracy and clarity.
