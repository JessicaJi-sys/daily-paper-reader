Abstract 

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models scale parameter count beyond a token’s active compute by routing each token to K experts drawn from a much larger pool of N ≫ K. This raises a basic question: does MoE’s benefit come from the dynamic, learned combination of experts, or specifically from the combinatorial capacity of selecting from a large pool? We isolate the first factor with a fully dense analogue: a fixed set of K complete SwiGLU experts, all active on every token, combined by a per-token softmax gate, with no larger pool ( N = K)and no discretization, at fixed total FFN width, so the dense baseline is the K = 1 case of the same family. Validation loss is non-monotonic in K: K = 2 finishes below the dense baseline (−0.0048 ), while K = 4 and K = 6 finish above it ( +0 .0053 and +0 .0197 ), with the gap to the baseline growing with K. All three expert models start behind the baseline early in training; 

K = 2 overtakes it between steps 4000 and 4500 and stays ahead for the rest of training, while K = 4 and K = 6 never do, and all three gaps plateau by roughly step 9000. Router diagnostics mostly rule out collapse: averaged over layers, per-token gate entropy is moderate (≈ 0.66 –0.72 normalized) and expert usage is fairly balanced ( ≥ 0.90 normalized) for every K.One layer is a clear exception: in the K = 4 model, layer 0’s gate is nearly one-hot (91% of tokens have a gate weight above 0.9, usage 93 /1/5/1% across experts), while every other layer in every model stays soft. Pairwise cosine similarity between that model’s expert outputs is far higher in layer 0 (mean 0.41) and in the last layer (mean 0.39) than in any layer between them (0.02–0.16), plausibly reflecting each layer’s structural position (proximity to the embedding; convergence toward the output head) rather than functional interchangeability: forcing layer 0’s gate to uniform at evaluation increases loss by 2.4 nats on a fixed diagnostic subset, showing its collapsed routing is load-bearing, not redundant in any sense that makes it safe to override. We also show that the architecture is exactly a dense SwiGLU of width Kd e whose hidden units are scaled by token-dependent, simplex-constrained group weights, so the dense baseline is contained in its function class. These results are consistent with a granularity sweet spot for a softly gated, always-active FFN at fixed total width, with K = 2 best among the values tested and larger K costing more than it buys; we report this from single runs per configuration. 

# 1 Introduction 

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts architectures have become a standard way to decouple a language model’s total parameter count from its per-token compute. A router selects the top-K of N experts for each token, with N often an order of magnitude or more larger than K [3, 5, 20], so only the 1arXiv:2610.02584v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 selected experts’ parameters are used. Making this practical requires solving a set of systems and optimization problems: load-balancing auxiliary losses, expert-capacity limits, token dropping under imbalance, and all-to-all communication across expert-parallel devices. This machinery exists to make routing discrete and therefore cheap; evaluating all N experts densely would defeat the purpose. Discreteness is a computational convenience, not obviously a source of quality. The original Mixture-of-Experts formulation [7] used a fully dense, differentiable gate over all experts, and sparse routing was introduced later to make large expert pools afford-able [20]. This leaves a question distinct from “does MoE help” and from “does a larger total pool help at fixed active compute” (both well supported [22]): does the dynamic per-token combination mecha-nism itself provide any benefit, independent of pool size? We test this with the simplest instance: a fixed, small set of K complete experts, all active on every token, combined by a learned softmax gate. There is no routing, no discretization, and no larger pool ( N = K). The architecture is matched to a dense SwiGLU transformer in parameters and FLOPs, and it generalizes the baseline: the gate is near-uniform at initialization and can, in principle, learn behavior close to a single FFN. In the worst case we would expect parity. Validation loss turns out to be non-monotonic in K at fixed total FFN width, with the dense baseline as K = 1 : K = 2 ends below the baseline, and K = 4 , 6 end above it by an increasing margin (Section 5). All expert models trail the baseline early in training, and only K = 2 overtakes it, doing so partway through training and holding a stable lead afterward, while K = 4 , 6 never overtake it and instead plateau at a persistent deficit. Router diagnostics mostly rule out collapse: entropy and usage stay moderate to balanced for almost every layer of every model, with one clear exception, a single layer in the K = 4 model whose gate is nearly one-hot. We also show the architecture is exactly a dense wide FFN with token-dependent, group-wise scaling of hidden units, which contains the baseline as a special case; this frames the K sweep as a granularity study of that one added mechanism. Our contributions are: 1. A controlled, FLOP- and parameter-matched comparison between a dense SwiGLU trans-former and a dense transformer with an always-active, dynamically-weighted set of K experts, at small scale. 2. An exact algebraic equivalence (Section 3.3): the model is a dense SwiGLU of width Kd e with token-dependent, simplex-constrained group scaling of hidden units, so the dense baseline is the K = 1 case of the same family. 3. A methodological caution: an early conclusion that the baseline wins, drawn from single-batch logged training loss during training, did not hold on validation loss at the end of annealing. 4. A granularity sweep at fixed total FFN width: K = 2 finishes below the dense baseline in validation loss, while K = 4 and K = 6 finish above it by a growing margin; only K = 2 ’s loss curve crosses the baseline’s during training, while K = 4 , 6 never do and instead plateau at a persistent deficit. 5. Router diagnostics for K = 2 , 4, 6, layer by layer: gates are mostly soft and experts fairly evenly used, with one clear exception, a single collapsed layer in the K = 4 model, which a layer-averaged summary would miss. 6. Practical guidance for small-scale MoE-inspired research, where details of this kind can reverse an apparent result. 22 Related Work 

Classical dense-gated and sparse MoE. The original MoE formulation [7] uses a differentiable gate over all experts, every expert processes every input, and outputs are combined by the gate distribution. This is the direct ancestor of the design studied here. Sparse top-k routing was introduced to make much larger expert pools tractable [20]; GShard [8] and Switch Transformer [5] developed the load-balancing and capacity machinery, and ST-MoE [26] introduced the router z-loss for stability. Fine-grained expert segmentation [2, 3] pushes N far beyond K (DeepSeek-V3 uses 256 routed experts with 8 active plus a shared expert), relying on combinatorial selection to keep narrow experts expressive. We return to the pre-sparse formulation deliberately, as an instrument for isolating what dynamic combination contributes independent of pool size. 

Soft routing via token merging. Soft MoE [18] avoids discrete routing differently: it forms per-expert “slots” as learned weighted averages over all tokens in the sequence , processes each slot with its expert, and reconstructs token outputs as weighted combinations over slots. This is a different axis of “softness” from ours (mixing tokens before routing, rather than mixing expert outputs per token), and the authors note that it is difficult to apply to autoregressive decoders because slot computation normalizes over the whole sequence; they leave this to future work. Our gate is per-token with no cross-token dependency and is therefore causal by construction. 

Soft routing via parameter merging. SMEAR [14] constructs a single merged expert whose parameters are the router-weighted average of the experts’ parameters, then runs one forward pass through it, keeping compute close to that of a single expert. For a nonlinear expert f ,

Geometric and theoretical analyses of soft routing. The closest empirical setup we found compares dense, top-k, and fully-soft routing under matched capacity, but through representational geometry (Jacobian spectra, effective rank) rather than training dynamics or task loss [11]; its fully-soft configuration is architecturally close to ours. On the theory side, convergence guarantees exist for jointly trained soft-routed MoE with nonlinear routers and experts under weighted-sum aggregation [10]. Whether the expert redundancy we observe (Section 5.5) matches the feature-learning behavior these analyses predict is beyond our scope. 

Causality-preserving differentiable routing. A differentiable soft top-k relaxation [23] keeps routing per-token (hence causal) while relaxing the hard top-k operator, with a learned per-layer expert budget. (It shares the short name “SoftMoE” with, but is unrelated to, the method of Puigcerver et al.) It still targets genuine sparsity, whereas our design is dense over a small fixed K.

Granularity, dense training, and FFN splitting. Granularity, the ratio of expert width to dense FFN width, was introduced as an explicit hyperparameter for sparse MoE, and the common choice of setting expert size equal to the dense FFN width was found to be suboptimal at almost any compute budget [13]. At larger scale, sparse MoE with an activation rate in a suitable range is reported to beat a dense model with the same total parameters, compute and data [9]. Other work uses dense computation inside MoE training: DS-MoE trains with all experts active and routes sparsely only at inference [15], and a lightweight method gives the router a dense gradient while 3keeping sparse activation [16]. On the FFN side, the sum of parallel FFNs with a shared input is equivalent to one wider FFN [1], which matches our uniform-gate case; MoEfication splits a pretrained FFN into k experts of width dff /k to sparsify it [25]; and a multi-branch FFN variant in machine translation was reported to slightly hurt performance [4]. None of these studies sweeps the number of groups of an always-active, softly gated FFN at fixed total width. 

Positioning. Within the searches we ran (dense-gated MoE, soft routing, granularity studies, FFN splitting), we did not find prior work that varies the number of groups K of a softly gated, always-active FFN at fixed total width, with the dense baseline as K = 1 , and analyzes it through an exact equivalence to a wide dense FFN. Prior soft-routing work either targets large N with capacity gains as the goal, targets single-expert FLOPs, or studies geometry.

Training. Sequences of 1024 tokens are packed from concatenated documents (no padding; loss and attention use only a causal mask), tokenized with the GPT-2 BPE vocabulary (50,257 tokens). Each optimizer step uses a micro-batch of 16 sequences with 4 gradient-accumulation steps, giving an effective batch of 64 sequences (65,536 tokens) per step; batches are not shuffled, so all models see identical data in identical order. The schedule has 500 warmup steps, in which the learning rate grows exponentially from 8 × 10 −5 by a factor of 1.0025 per step to a peak of ≈ 2.8 × 10 −4, followed by 20,000 steps of cosine annealing to 10 −5. Training covers 1,343,488,000 tokens, about 1.03 passes over the ∼1.3B-token training subset (so close to a single epoch). Training data is a subsample of FineWeb-10B [17]; validation uses the full FineWeb validation split. Throughout this paper, “step” refers to the optimizer step count after the 500-step warmup (i.e. step s in our tables and figures is the (s+500) -th optimizer step from initialization); token counts were logged from true step 0 and are unaffected by this offset. Optimization uses AdamW [12] with weight decay 0.01 (PyTorch defaults otherwise: β1=0 .9, β2=0 .999 , ϵ=10 −8), mixed fp16 precision, on 2 ×NVIDIA T4 GPUs. No random seed was fixed, so each of the four runs used an independent, uncontrolled initialization and data-loader state; this is part of why we treat the final-loss gaps in Section 5 as suggestive pending a noise estimate (Section 4, Limitations). 

Compared models. (i) Dense SwiGLU baseline; (ii) dense dynamic MoE (this work) with K ∈{2, 4, 6}, all at fixed total FFN width 4dmodel (Table 1). 

Metrics. We compare models by validation loss, logged every 500 steps on the full FineWeb validation split, disjoint from training. Logged training loss is the single-batch loss at the evaluation step and is too noisy to compare across runs, so we do not use it for comparison (Section 5 discusses a case where it was misleading). Each configuration was run once, without seed-based replication (Limitations).

The router’s concentration of 93% of its weight on a single expert at layer 0 is better read as having found a specific, necessary transformation, which uniform blending with three imperfectly-aligned alternatives corrupts rather than approximates. Why layer 11, under a plausibly similar structural pressure toward alignment, did not also collapse remains unexplained. 

# 6 Discussion 

Why is there a sweet spot at K = 2 ? By Section 3.3, the only thing the gate adds over a wide dense FFN is a token-dependent, simplex-constrained scaling of K groups of hidden units; the dense baseline is the K=1 case where that scaling is trivial (always 1). Two groups with a soft, mostly-balanced gate (Table 4) is enough to beat the baseline slightly; four or six groups is not, and gets worse as K grows. One reading is a trade-off: splitting the same total width into more, narrower groups gives the gate more degrees of freedom to reweight, but each group is individually less capable 10 (Section 5.3), and past K=2 the second cost outgrows the first benefit. This is consistent with, but not established by, our data; it predicts that expert outputs should be more similar (redundant) at K=2 than at K=4 , 6, and that a static-weight (non-input-dependent) version of K=2 should still beat the baseline by less than the full dynamic version, since it lacks the reweighting benefit but keeps the narrower groups. Cosine similarity for the K=4 model (Section 5.5.1) shows the redundancy pattern is not uniform across depth (concentrated in the first and last layers) and is not, by itself, sufficient to explain the one router collapse we observe. This trade-off account remains a hypothesis rather than an established mechanism. 

The K=4 layer-0 collapse. That one layer, in one configuration, converges to near-hard routing without any mechanism designed to produce that (Section 5.5) is itself notable: it shows this architecture can rediscover something close to discrete top-1 selection on its own, at least locally, purely from gradient descent on a continuous softmax. We initially hypothesized that layer 0’s elevated expert-output cosine similarity (Section 5.5.1) made its collapse inexpensive, since near-duplicate experts would make the router’s precise weighting inconsequential. A direct ablation (Section 5.5.2) rules this out: forcing layer 0’s gate to uniform costs roughly 2.4 nats of validation loss, an order-of-magnitude increase in perplexity, so its collapsed routing is load-bearing rather than safe to override. The elevated cosine similarity is better read as a structural, position-dependent alignment (Section 5.5.2) than as functional redundancy. Why this collapse happens in layer 0 and not the similarly aligned layer 11 remains unexplained. 

What the result implies about MoE. Because the expert models have the same FLOPs and parameters as the baseline and at least the same function class (Section 3.3), the differences we observe (all within about 0.02 validation loss) cannot come from extra capacity in the sense sparse MoE has it. This is consistent with the sparse-MoE literature’s attribution of its benefit to selecting from a larger pool ( N ≫ K) [2, 9, 13], which this design cannot do by construction. 

Practical advantages retained. The design needs no load-balancing loss, capacity factor, or token dropping, and it is causal and incrementally decodable (unlike token-merging Soft MoE), independent of whether K=2 ’s small win holds up under further scrutiny. 

# 7 Limitations 

Scale is small (28–29M non-embedding, 48M total parameters, Table 1) and results come from one dataset. Each configuration was run once, with no random seed fixed, so the four runs are independent single draws rather than seed-paired comparisons, and the −0.0048 /+0 .0053 /+0 .0197 

gaps in Table 3 are single-run figures; we read the stable plateaus in Figure 2 as evidence they reflect the architecture rather than noise, but this is not established by seed-based replication. The K=4 layer-0 collapse and its ablation come from a single checkpoint, with no training-time trajectory. The layer-0 ablation used a smaller, fixed validation subset for computational reasons (Section 5.5.2); its within-ablation loss increase, not its absolute values, is what is comparable to the rest of the paper. The trade-off account of the K=2 sweet spot (Section 6) is a hypothesis, not an established mechanism. We did not train a sparse MoE model or test K values beyond {1, 2, 4, 6}.These results are specific to this scale, dataset, and architecture. 11 8 Conclusion 

At fixed total FFN width, splitting a dense SwiGLU into a small, always-active, dynamically-weighted set of K experts is not uniformly good or bad: K=2 slightly beats the dense baseline (K=1 ) in validation loss and overtakes it partway through training, while K=4 and K=6 fall increasingly behind and never overtake it, with all three gaps stable over the last 11,000 of 20,000 training steps. Router diagnostics mostly show soft, balanced gates, ruling out collapse as a general explanation, but one layer of the K=4 model collapses toward near-hard routing on its own, without any mechanism encouraging it to. An exact equivalence to a wide dense FFN with token-dependent group scaling of hidden units (Section 3.3) frames this as a granularity study: adding one soft reweighting dimension to a fixed FFN budget can help a little, in moderation, and hurt increasingly once experts are split too finely, with no larger expert pool available to compensate. These results come from single runs per configuration, without seed-based replication, and should be read with that scope in mind.
