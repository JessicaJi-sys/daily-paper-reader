Abstract 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) layers increase model capacity without a proportional increase in per-example computation. However, conventional flat routers can yield imbalanced expert utilization and treat experts as an unstructured collection, whose indices carry no topological meaning. We introduce BRANCH-MoE , a routing architecture that places E

experts at the leaves of a binary decision tree of depth log 2 E. At each internal node the branching probability is centered on the arrival-weighted mean score of the traffic reaching that node. This mean is estimated using an exponential moving average, which promotes utilization of both child subtrees without an auxiliary load-balancing loss. We show that this moving-average estimate admits an explicit noise–lag trade-off. We prove that for linear node maps and log-concave arrival distributions, this mechanism prevents routing-mass collapse. We further establish that, under a frozen router, an expert’s execution frequency controls its stochastic-gradient convergence rate, and that confident decisions near the root bound cross-device communication when experts are assigned to devices by tree prefix. We evaluate BRANCH-MoE against Switch softmax, DeepSeek-V3 dynamic-bias, Skywork logit-normalized, and deterministic hash routing on Criteo click-through-rate prediction, Forest Covertype, HIGGS, and YearPredictionMSD, using E = 16, top-4 routing, and five random seeds. BRANCH-MoE matches the predictive performance of the strongest baselines, attaining the lowest mean held-out loss on Forest Covertype and showing no statistically significant difference from the best-performing methods on Criteo, HIGGS, or YearPredictionMSD across random seeds. It also maintains balanced expert load without an auxiliary penalty. Unlike flat routers, BRANCH-MoE induces input-conditional co-routing locality: for each input token, the experts selected together tend to lie closer to one another in the routing tree. On all three UCI benchmarks, linear node maps yield a normalized tree distance of 0 .710–0 .718 between co-selected experts, compared with 0 .817 for random expert pairs—a reduction of 12–13%. Every flat-routing baseline remains within 3 .5% of the random-pair distance. Together, these results show that hierarchical routing can preserve task quality and balanced utilization while inducing a topology that supports localized expert co-activation and reduced communication. 

# 1 Introduction 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE; Jacobs et al., 1991) layers increase parameter capacity while activating only a small subset of expert networks for each token (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2022). A standard token-choice layer uses a flat E-way router followed by top-K

selection. This design has enabled very large sparse models, but it leaves several properties to losses, constraints, or systems heuristics rather than building them into the routing geometry. 

Limitations of flat routing. First, a flat softmax or top-K gate provides no distribution-free positive lower bound on the mass assigned to every expert: small initial routing advantages can produce highly unequal update frequencies, and an underused expert, receiving little data, can 1arXiv:2610.06725v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 improve slowly (Eigen et al., 2013; Shazeer et al., 2017). Auxiliary load-balancing losses, capacity constraints, balanced assignment, and expert-choice routing mitigate this problem (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Lewis et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022), but the protection does not follow from the geometry of a flat gate. Second, a flat expert label is an unstructured categorical index that does not encode which experts share a device, node, rack, or other hardware cut, so the router offers no prefix locality for a distributed implementation to exploit. Third, preventing early routing collapse requires an auxiliary load-balancing penalty that competes with the task objective or an external bias controller (Wang et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2024). We do not claim that every conventional MoE collapses or incurs the same communication cost, only that these guarantees do not arise from flat routing alone. Our proposal, BRANCH-MoE, changes the routing architecture itself. Its novelty is the combination of three coupled mechanisms within one routing tree: (i) a depth-D binary routing tree for E = 2 D experts; (ii) EMA score anchors and induced offsets that center every routing decision on the tokens arriving at its node; and (iii) a routing-mask cascade whose prefixes can serve as hierarchical expert or device addresses. The cascade determines the incoming mask used to update each node’s anchor, the anchor determines the routing offset, and the final mask selects the experts each token is dispatched to, with the tree path giving each leaf a structured binary address rather than an arbitrary flat label. 

Our contributions. We summarize our contributions as follows: 1. Architecture. BRANCH-MoE couples a soft mask cascade with one scalar EMA score anchor per node, updated with mask weights and a single shared decay α (Section 3). The anchor induces the inference-time bias bj = −νj , so no auxiliary balancing loss, separately learned bias, or d-dimensional centroid is needed; the full soft router evaluates E − 1 internal nodes in O(log 2 E) sequential depth, and inference uses the stored anchors as fixed biases without EMA updates. 2. Theory (Section 4). (1) The mask-weighted constant-decay EMA tracks each node’s incoming mean score with an explicit noise–lag trade-off. (2) For linear routing under log-concave arrival distributions, anchoring prevents vanishing expected branch mass and yields a positive soft leaf-mass bound that degrades quantitatively with anchor error. (3) Under a frozen router and strongly convex conditional expert risks, the realized expert-execution frequency controls the convergence rate of expert SGD (the soft-mass bound does not itself guarantee top-K

execution). (4) We formalize the communication locality induced by tree routing: a token’s experts are determined by its path, so co-routed tokens share path prefixes and confident upper-level decisions concentrate a token’s routing mass within a shared-prefix subtree; under tree-aligned expert placement, the depth at which two paths diverge bounds whether their dispatches cross a hardware boundary, giving explicit bounds on cross-domain communication (Section 4.4). 3. Experiments. We compare BRANCH-MoE against flat softmax (Switch), auxiliary-loss-free (DeepSeek-V3), logit-normalized (Skywork; Wei et al., 2024), and deterministic hash routing on four tabular benchmarks, namely Criteo click-through-rate prediction (Criteo AI Lab, 2015), Forest Covertype (Blackard & Dean, 1999; Blackard, 1998), HIGGS (Baldi et al., 2014; Whiteson, 2014), and YearPredictionMSD (Bertin-Mahieux et al., 2011; Bertin-Mahieux, 2011) (see Appendix D for description of datasets). As experiments show BRANCH-MoE matches the strongest baselines, with the lowest mean held-out loss on Forest Covertype and ties within seed noise on Criteo, HIGGS, and MSD. On all three UCI benchmarks, it is the only router whose co-routing statistics fall materially below the analytical random-pair baseline, reducing normalized tree distance to 0 .710–0 .718 against 0 .817 (12–13% lower) and the leaf index gap by 18–23%, while every flat router stays within 3 .5% of random (Table 2). 22 Background and Related Work

Switch Transformer specializes it to top-1 routing and combines an auxiliary load-balancing loss with fixed expert capacities (Fedus et al., 2022). DeepSeek-V3 instead adjusts expert selection through a dynamic per-expert bias: the bias increases for underloaded experts and decreases for overloaded experts, while the gate multiplying the expert output is computed from the original, unbiased affinity score (DeepSeek-AI, 2024). Its primary balancing mechanism is therefore auxiliary-loss-free. However, its implementation includes a complementary sequence-wise balancing loss to encourage an even distribution of expert load within each sequence. BRANCH-MoE also uses routing biases, but for a different purpose. Its bias bj = −νj is the negated EMA of the arrival-weighted mean score at node j, and anchors that node’s decision boundary at the center of its own incoming score distribution; it is not a load-error feedback variable. Other methods alter the assignment rule more fundamentally. BASE constructs a balanced batch assignment through a matching problem (Lewis et al., 2021), while Sinkhorn-BASE approximates such an assignment using entropy-regularized optimal transport and Sinkhorn iterations (Clark et al., 2022). Expert Choice reverses the usual assignment direction: each expert selects a fixed-size bucket of high-scoring tokens, controlling expert workloads while allowing different tokens to be processed by different numbers of experts (Zhou et al., 2022). Hash Layers remove the learned router and assign inputs to experts using a fixed input-dependent hash, thereby avoiding router parameters, balancing losses, and batchwise matching (Roller et al., 2021). These methods respectively impose balance through batch-coupled assignment, expert-side capacity control, or a fixed routing rule. BRANCH-MoE instead retains learned, representation-dependent token routing without solving a globally coupled assignment problem. BRANCH-MoE is closely related to hierarchical conditional-computation architectures, including hierarchical mixtures of experts (Jordan & Jacobs, 1994; Shazeer et al., 2017), soft decision trees ( ˙Irsoy et al., 2012; Frosst & Hinton, 2017), tree-based sparse gating mechanisms (Ibrahim et al., 2023), and fast feedforward networks (Belcak & Wattenhofer, 2023). Hierarchical mixtures of experts arrange routing decisions in a tree structure (Jordan & Jacobs, 1994). Similarly, the soft decision tree of Frosst and Hinton uses learned sigmoid splits and path probabilities to distill a neural network into a tree whose leaves represent class distributions (Frosst & Hinton, 2017). BRANCH-MoE employs the hierarchy differently: its soft, fully differentiable branch decisions route tokens to trainable neural experts, and the same cascaded masks used for routing also determine the tokens used to update each node’s score anchor. Notably, BRANCH-MoE does not enforce exact per-batch load equality, as do assignment-constrained methods. Instead, under the conditions stated in our analysis, its anchored hierarchy provides a population-level nonstarvation guarantee while preserving adaptive sparse routing. # 5 Experiments 

We evaluate BRANCH-MoE against representative MoE routers on four benchmarks, testing (i) whether it is competitive on task quality without an auxiliary balancing loss and (ii) whether it induces the expert locality analyzed in Section 4.4. In short, quality is at parity with the strongest baselines on all four benchmarks, locality appears on the three UCI benchmarks but not on Criteo, and load stays balanced without an auxiliary loss. 

5.1 Setup 

We use Criteo ( ≈41 .5M examples, 39 fields), Forest Covertype (581K, 54 features), HIGGS (600K, 28), and YearPredictionMSD (515K, 90). All MoE conditions share one block of E = 16 SwiGLU experts (top-4, capacity factor 1 .25) between a minimal input stage and a linear head, and differ only in the gate. The baselines are softmax top-K with an auxiliary loss (Switch), dynamic-bias routing (DeepSeek-V3), logit-normalized softmax with an auxiliary loss (Skywork), and a hash-based control (Hash). Node maps are linear or two-layer MLPs of width h ∈ { 2, 4, 8}.Every condition has five seeds; comparisons are paired by seed on UCI and unpaired (Welch) on Criteo, where TPU execution is not bitwise reproducible. We report the normalized tree distance 

L/D between co-selected experts ( L = bit length( i ⊕ j), D = log 2 E; random pairs score 0 .817, lower is more local) and the load CV (std/mean of per-expert load). Details: Section B.

Locality. On the three UCI benchmarks BRANCH-MoE is the only router materially below random (Table 2b). Balance. BRANCH-MoE uses no auxiliary loss or bias controller. On UCI its linear-map load CV (0 .09–0 .23) is comparable to that of the auxiliary-loss routers at their best maps (Switch 0.10–0 .28, Skywork 0 .14–0 .54); on Covertype the only dead experts are Skywork’s at h = 2 (6 of 80 slots). On Criteo its CV of 0 .115 exceeds the flat routers’ ( ≤ 0.006) but costs nothing measurable: it drops 0 .08% of dispatches. 

Node maps. The node map can matter more than the routing rule (Figure 2a). On Covertype, replacing the linear map by an h = 8 MLP lowers cross-entropy by 0 .017–0 .028 for every learned 13 router (paired t from −7.99 to −21 .44), 3 .5–5 .8× the largest gap between routers at h = 8 (0 .0049; 0 .016 at linear), while Hash, which ignores the node map, moves by only 0 .00013. The sign depends on the dataset: MSD agrees; on HIGGS the linear map is best for Skywork and DeepSeek-V3 and ties for BRANCH-MoE; on Criteo no router’s loss changes significantly (≤ 4.1 × 10 −4, p > 0.1) except Switch’s, which fails with every MLP map ( ≈68% of dispatches dropped). The MLP also loosens BRANCH-MoE’s structure: on UCI its tree distance rises by 0.024–0 .031 (Figure 2b; same sign on all three datasets, not resolved on Covertype alone), and on Criteo its load CV rises from 0 .115 to 0 .21–0 .38 ( p ≤ 0.01 at h = 4 and 8). 

Takeaway. Balance without an auxiliary loss and locality on UCI are supported, and quality is at parity; locality on Criteo and whether MLP maps erode locality remain open (Section C). 

Conclusion and limitations. BRANCH-MoE combines arrival-weighted score anchoring with hierarchical routing to encourage balanced population-level utilization and structured per-token expert selection without an auxiliary balancing loss. Our analysis characterizes anchor tracking, establishes soft-mass anti-collapse for linear routing under log-concave arrivals, relates realized expert-selection frequency to optimization progress, and bounds communication locality under prefix-aligned placement. The experiments demonstrate competitive task quality and structured locality across several benchmarks. The present study is limited to a single BRANCH-MoE layer and therefore does not capture interactions among routing decisions across multiple layers. Future work will extend both the theory and empirical evaluation to multilayer architectures and develop fused, topology-aware implementations for large-scale distributed settings. 14 AI use statement

Pinning any column with adapted variance below 10 −4 to unit variance eliminates the collapse and reduces the seed-to-seed accuracy spread of BRANCH-MoE from 6 .2 to 0 .46 percentage points. 

B.2 Baselines 

We benchmark against four representative MoE routing paradigms, covering auxiliary-loss, auxiliary-loss-free, normalization-based, and deterministic mechanisms. 1. Top-K Softmax Routing (Switch). The canonical learned paradigm (Fedus et al., 2022): a learned gating matrix, a softmax, and top-K dispatch, with multiplicative uniform jitter [1 − ϵ, 1 + ϵ], ϵ = 10 −2, during training. Load balance is enforced by the auxiliary loss 

Lbalance = αE P 

> e

fePe with α = 0 .01. 2. Auxiliary-Loss-Free Routing (DeepSeek-V3). Affinities are computed with an inde-pendent sigmoid per expert, so experts do not compete for probability mass (DeepSeek-AI, 2024). A non-trainable additive bias be enters top-K selection only and is updated after each step by a fixed increment γ = 10 −3 in the direction that relieves congestion; the combining gates remain the unbiased affinities. 3. Gating Logit Normalization (Skywork). Logits are standardized across the expert dimension before the softmax, which counteracts representation collapse and sharpens the gate distribution (Wei et al., 2024). A fixed auxiliary balancing coefficient is retained. We include this baseline because it proves to be the strongest competitor on two of the four benchmarks and ties Switch as the runner-up on a third. 4. Deterministic Balanced Routing (Hash). A fixed assignment mapping each example to experts by a 32-bit avalanche hash of its feature vector, following Hash Layers (Roller et al., 2021). The assignment is uniform by construction but carries no dependence on content beyond the hash, isolating the value of learned gating against a perfectly balanced but semantically uninformed one, at zero router parameter cost. 5. BRANCH-MoE (ours). The E experts are the leaves of a balanced binary tree of depth log 2 E. At internal node n the probability of branching right is σ(fn(x) − μn). During training μn is the arrival-weighted mean score of the current batch at n; at inference it is νn, a 21 non-trainable exponential moving average (decay 0 .99) of that batch mean, updated at every training step. Centering each split on the mean score of its own traffic places the decision boundary through the middle of the distribution actually arriving there, keeping both children populated without a balancing penalty. Leaf scores are products of branch probabilities along each root-to-leaf path; all E − 1 node scores are evaluated, and the products are formed in log 2 E vectorized steps.

Architecture and protocol. To isolate routing, we adopt a minimal backbone: an input stage, a single MoE block, and a linear head. On Criteo, the input stage projects the 39 embeddings to 256 dimensions, followed by SiLU and layer normalization; on the UCI datasets, it is a feature-standardization layer alone with no projection. The MoE block has no residual connection. Within this block, each expert is a SwiGLU network defined as down(SiLU(gate( x)) ⊙ up( x)), where the up layer expands the input into a higher-dimensional space and the down layer compresses it back to the baseline hidden size. Finally, a post-MoE layer normalization precedes the head on Criteo, HIGGS, and MSD (the Covertype model has none). All conditions use E = 16 and top-4 routing with capacity factor 1 .25, and differ only in the gate g(·), so comparisons are at matched capacity. Criteo trains for 37 ,500 steps at batch 2048 with Adam (lr = 5 × 10 −4), organized as 15 evaluation intervals of 2 ,500 steps; since an interval covers 5 .12M examples against a ≈37M training split, this is ≈2.1 passes over the data rather than 15. The UCI datasets train for 20 true epochs with Adam (lr = 10 −3) at batch 512 (Covertype) or 256 (HIGGS, MSD). Every condition is replicated over 5 seeds. On the UCI benchmarks the seed governs initialization but not the data order, which is shuffled without a seed; all arms within a dataset share the identical seed set, so comparisons are paired by seed. On Criteo the seed likewise governs initialization only, and TPU execution is not bitwise reproducible, so Criteo comparisons are unpaired (two-sided Welch t-tests). Re-running an identical Criteo configuration with the same seed changes held-out logloss about as much as changing the seed does (standard deviation 1 .5–2 .8 × 10 −4 within a seed against 1 .9–3 .0 × 10 −4

across seeds).

# D Description of Datasets 

We evaluate the considered methods on four widely used tabular benchmarks spanning binary classification, multiclass classification, and regression. These datasets vary substantially in size, feature type, and prediction task. 

Criteo. The Criteo 1TB Click Logs dataset is a large-scale benchmark for click-through-rate (CTR) prediction. Each instance corresponds to a display advertisement served by Criteo, and the binary label indicates whether the ad was clicked. The training data consist of 24 chronologically ordered daily files and contain 13 numerical, predominantly count-based features and 26 anonymized categorical features. The categorical values are hashed into 32-bit integers, and some entries may be missing. We use this dataset as a large-scale binary classification task (Criteo AI Lab, 2015). 

Forest Covertype. The Forest Covertype dataset consists of 581 ,012 observations of 30 × 30 meter forest patches from the Roosevelt National Forest in Colorado. The task is to predict the dominant forest cover type among seven classes from 54 cartographic variables, including elevation, slope, aspect, hillshade measurements, wilderness-area indicators, and soil-type indicators. It therefore provides a multiclass classification benchmark with a mix of continuous and binary features (Blackard & Dean, 1999; Blackard, 1998). 

HIGGS. The HIGGS dataset is a binary classification dataset derived from simulated high-energy particle-collision events. Its goal is to distinguish events corresponding to a signal process that produces Higgs bosons from background events. The dataset contains 11 million instances, each represented by 28 real-valued features: 21 low-level kinematic variables measured by particle detectors and seven high-level features derived from the low-level variables. This dataset is a standard large-scale benchmark for binary classification (Baldi et al., 2014; Whiteson, 2014). 

YearPredictionMSD. The YearPredictionMSD dataset is a regression benchmark constructed from the Million Song Dataset. The task is to predict a song’s release year from 90 audio features that summarize timbre characteristics. It contains 515 ,345 songs, primarily Western commercial recordings released between 1922 and 2011. We use it to assess performance on a large-scale continuous prediction problem (Bertin-Mahieux et al., 2011; Bertin-Mahieux, 2011). 26
