Abstract 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) routers typically apply softmax to the router scores and keep the top-K experts, making every token use exactly K experts. Sparsity-inducing probability maps such as sparsemax, α-entmax and normmax can adaptively assign exact zeros to selected experts, and therefore appear to offer token-dependent expert partici-pation, even when using the same top-K machinery. In this work, we study whether and how this sparsity survives training. We train matched 300M and 1B top-2 MoE language models with softmax, 1.5-entmax, sparsemax and 

2-normmax, and find that the maps behave very differently once trained: at 1B, entmax discards 30% less probability mass than softmax while almost never dropping a selected expert, sparsemax retains the most mass, and normmax routes 21% of tokens to a single expert. These outcomes are not properties of the maps alone. Each map drops a selected expert only when the gap between the two largest scores reaches a fixed threshold, and the trained routers differ in the score distribution they learn: the entmax router learns scores with roughly half the spread of softmax’s, which keeps its top-2 gaps below its threshold, while sparsemax and normmax, which share the same threshold, learn different gap distributions and hence different participation. Routers thus co-adapt their scores to the map, and a map’s capacity to produce zeros does not by itself determine expert participation. While none of the sparse maps improves validation loss over softmax, they make the trained models far less sensitive to selecting more experts at inference: sparsemax trained with K=2 loses 0.02 nats when run with K=8 , where softmax loses 0.58 . Our results indicate that adaptive MoE routing has to be designed around the joint behavior of the probability map and the learned scores, rather than around the map alone. 1

# 1 Introduction 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) layers scale model capacity by activating only a few expert networks per token (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2024). A router scores the experts, a probability map turns the scores into weights, and a top-K operator keeps the K highest-weighted experts, whose outputs are combined with the renormalized weights. With softmax, every token uses exactly K experts, regardless of how many it needs, and the selected experts often account for only a fraction of the router’s probability mass—the rest is discarded by truncation. Sparsity-inducing probability maps promise a way out without changing the top-K machinery. Sparsemax (Martins and Astudillo, 2016), α-entmax (Peters et al., 2019) and normmax (Santos et al., 2024; 2025) can assign exact zeros , so a selected expert may receive zero weight and drop out of the mixture. The number of experts that actually contribute to a token is then data-dependent , even when the expert budget K stays fixed. Several recent routers also build on this idea (Oldfield et al., 2024; Thaman, 2025; Wang et al., 2025). However, how sparsity behaves in these models is not obvious. Since the router scores are learned, changing the map also changes the scores that the map receives: a trained sparsemax router and a trained softmax router differ both in how they transform scores and in which scores they learned to produce. As a result, a map that can produce zeros may rarely do so once its router has been trained

In this paper, we study whether the sparsity of the probability map survives training, and what determines expert participation when it does not. We train matched 300M and 1B top-2 MoE language models with softmax, 1.5-entmax, sparsemax and 2-normmax under an identical architecture, data and balancing objective, and we measure the probability mass retained by the selected experts ( retention ), the number of selected experts with non-zero weight (participation ), and predictive quality. To relate the map to the scores it receives, we record the router scores of every trained model and measure how their spread and top-2 gaps sit relative to each map’s sparsification threshold. Figure 1 summarizes the setting and the outcomes. We find that a map’s ability to produce zeros does not, by itself, determine how many experts a token uses. Our main contributions are: • A controlled study of sparse maps as MoE routers. We train top-2 MoE language models at two scales that differ only in the probability map, and measure retention, participation, load balance and predictive quality, together with the router scores of every trained model (§3). • Retention and participation are distinct outcomes. Under top-2 selection, mass is discarded only when more than two experts have positive probability, while participation drops only when a single expert does (Equation 7). # 3 Experimental setup 

Models and training. We train MoE language models at two scales with a Llama 3-style backbone (Grattafiori et al., 2024) in which every second feed-forward block is replaced by an MoE layer: a 300M model (8 layers, width 320, 4 MoE layers with 26 experts each) and a 1B model (9 layers, width 768, 4 MoE layers with 27 experts each). Every token selects K = 2 experts, following Mixtral’s selection and weighted combination (Jiang et al., 2024), without capacity constraints or token dropping; the 1B model has 302 M active parameters. The four maps share everything except π, including the balancing objective of Equation (5) with coefficient 0.01 , averaged over MoE layers and evaluated on local microbatches. A dense model of matching depth and width at each scale provides a reference with a larger active-parameter budget. All models are trained on DCLM-baseline (Li et al., 2024) for 10 B tokens ( 19 ,073 

updates of 524 ,288 tokens) with AdamW, cosine decay and bfloat16 mixed precision. Appendices A.1 and A.2 give the architecture details and the full training and evaluation recipe. 

Evaluation. For the original matched comparison, we evaluate the final checkpoint of every run, which is also the one with the lowest DCLM validation loss. Predictive quality is measured by validation loss, mean accuracy over eight OLMES tasks and LAMBADA, and LAMBADA word perplexity (Gu et al., 2025; Paperno et al., 2016). For each OLMES task and model, we use the higher accuracy of the multiple-choice and completion formulations, following the benchmark’s recommendation (Gu et al., 2025, §3.4). Routing diagnostics use 61 documents from the WikiText-103 test subset of Paloma (Magnusson et al., 2024) at context 

2,048 , giving 285 ,107 targets per MoE layer. All routing statistics are averaged with equal weight over the four MoE layers. For every trained model we also record the router scores of all four layers on these documents, from which we compute the score spreads and top-2 gap distributions of §4.3. 

# 4 Results 

We first check that the MoEs are competent models (§4.1), then ask whether the sparsity of the map survives training (§4.2), whether the differences between trained routers come from the map or from the scores learned under it (§4.3), and what the sparse maps buy at inference time (§4.4). We report both scales and focus the discussion on the 1B models. The 300M models show the same qualitative routing patterns, with their training curves and load statistics in Appendix C.4.

All three sparse maps drop contributors early on, but entmax recovers to almost exactly two contributors per token, whereas sparsemax and normmax settle at 1.93 and 1.79 . # 4.3 Is it the map, or the scores learned under it? 

The trained models differ both in the map and in the scores that the map receives, and the two are easy to separate because each map has a fixed sparsification threshold (see §2.1). Concretely, a selected expert is dropped exactly when the top-2 score gap is at least 2 for 1.5-entmax and at least 1 for sparsemax and normmax-2. The singleton rate of a trained model is therefore nothing but the fraction of its learned gaps that reach its map’s threshold. Figure 3 shows these gap distributions for the 1B models, with both thresholds marked in every panel. The numerical spreads and quantiles at both scales are retained in Table 7 in Appendix C.2. The learned scores turn out to matter as much as the map. All three sparse routers learned markedly smaller score spreads than the softmax router, with an RMS between 0.32 and 0.38 against 0.86 . Under a sparse map, this scale determines how often a score gap reaches the singleton threshold. For 1.5-entmax the consequence is decisive. Its 

99 th-percentile gap is 1.45 , well below the threshold of 2, so the trained router produces a singleton in only 0.05% 

of states, even though 7.8% of the softmax router’s gaps reach that threshold. Sparsemax and normmax-2 tell a complementary story. The two maps share the threshold 1, so on any given score vector they always agree on whether a token keeps one or two contributors, and yet the trained models differ by 0.14 contributors per token. The difference can only come from the scores the two routers learned, and it does: the median gap of the normmax-2 router is 0.61 

against 0.40 for the sparsemax router, and its gaps reach 1 three times as often. Taken together, 1.5-entmax keeps both experts because its threshold is the highest of the three maps and because its router learned scores that stay under it, while sparsemax and normmax-2 differ only in what their routers learned. Participation is thus set jointly by the map’s threshold and by the learned score distribution, and the router adapts the latter to the former.

# 4.4 What the sparse maps buy at inference time 

Although they do not improve quality, the sparse maps make the trained model much less sensitive to selecting more experts at inference. We carry that analysis in Table 3. When the models trained with K=2 are run with K=8 and frozen weights, softmax loses 0.58 nats, because it is forced to spread weight over six experts it never learned to combine. Sparsemax loses only 0.02 nats, since it keeps assigning zero weight to most of the extra slots and uses 4.3

experts on average at K=8 , and normmax-2 and 1.5-entmax fall in between. The robustness is one-sided: reducing the 7budget to K=1 costs every map about 0.10 nats. For every map the loss is lowest at the trained budget, so selecting more experts to retain more mass does not help either, which confirms that retention, participation, and quality are three distinct quantities. 

Load balance. Counting contributors rather than nominal selections also changes the picture of expert balance, as the last three columns of Table 2 show. The 1.5-entmax router has the lowest load CV under both conventions ( 0.338 ), and because almost all of its selections carry positive weight, excluding the fillers changes nothing for it. Normmax-2 is different: its nominal CV is only slightly above softmax’s ( 0.341 against 0.339 ), but once the fillers are excluded its contributor CV rises to 0.388 , so its load is less balanced than the nominal histogram suggests, and sparsemax is the least balanced under either convention. The balancing objective is one possible source of these differences. Because it uses the full distribution, an expert with zero probability receives no direct balancing gradient under a sparse map, and changing the score scale also changes the probabilities seen by this objective. To examine the balancing mechanism, we trained 300M 1.5-entmax and sparsemax routers with softmax( z) used only in the auxiliary loss. Routing and expert combination still use their respective sparse maps. For 1.5-entmax, nominal and contributor CV both rise from 0.400 to 0.485 , while centered score RMS rises from 0.517 to 0.603 and the single-ton fraction falls from 0.295% to 0.079% . Sparsemax shows the same pattern. Nominal CV rises from 0.434 to 0.643 

and contributor CV from 0.450 to 0.668 (Table 2), while centered score RMS rises from 0.413 to 0.442 and the sin-gleton fraction falls from 9.598% to 7.941% . Since higher CV means a less even distribution of traffic across experts, the dense auxiliary probability does not improve load balance in either run. Figure 6 shows why fewer tokens become singletons: the entmax tail near g = 2 thins, and the sparsemax peak just above g = 1 becomes smaller. More tokens therefore keep both selected experts, but their contributions are distributed less evenly across the expert pool. Per-token participation and load balance move in opposite directions in both runs, which does not support the simple explanation that sparsemax’s imbalance comes solely from zero-probability experts receiving no direct balancing gradient. 

# 5 Related work 

Sparse maps and differentiable gates in MoE routers. Softmax followed by top-K remains the dominant router (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2024), with sigmoid scores renormalized among the selected experts as the main production alternative (DeepSeek-AI, 2024). Sparse probability maps have been used as routers before. For example, Multilinear MoE compares entmax with softmax over factorized experts (Oldfield et al., 2024), DUAL studies sparsemax under routing constraints (Thaman, 2025), and ReMoE replaces the map by rectified gates with a separate sparsity controller (Wang et al., 2025). Differentiable relaxations of the selection step itself include DSelect-k, a smooth gate with explicit cardinality control (Hazimeh et al., 2021), the sparse top-k operators of Sander et al. (2023), Soft MoE, which replaces discrete routing by soft slot assignment (Puigcerver et al., 2024), and, more recently, SoftMoE, a truncated LapSum relaxation with learned per-layer budgets (Zasada et al., 2026), and ProbMoE, which samples cardinality-constrained subsets and differentiates through inclusion marginals (Zhao et al., 2026). These works propose new routers and evaluate them end-to-end, whereas we keep the router fixed to top-2 and ask what a sparse map delivers once the router has been trained with it, and how the score distribution it learns interacts with the map’s sparsification threshold (§4.3). 

Load balancing and routing geometry. Balancing objectives range from the Switch-style loss we use (Fedus et al., 2022) to balanced assignment (Lewis et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022), loss-free bias adjustment (Wang et al., 2024) and ϕ-Balancing, which targets population-level balance through a convex potential and mirror descent (Chen et al., 2026). Yoon et al. (2026) show that the task gradient reaches only the selected experts and that balancing provides no token-level counterfactual signal, and Huu et al. (2026) analyze the switching surfaces of top-K routing and propose smoothing near them. Our observation that the balancing objective interacts with the map through the full distribution (§4.4) is complementary: it concerns which experts the balancing gradient can reach under a sparse map. 

Sparse probability transformations. Sparsemax (Martins and Astudillo, 2016) and α-entmax (Peters et al., 2019) are regularized argmax maps in the Fenchel–Young framework of Blondel et al. (2020), which also covers the norm entropies behind normmax (Santos et al., 2024; 2025). Their sparsity has been exploited in attention, where the support 8size is known to depend on the scale of the scores: Correia et al. (2019) learn α per head, and Vasylenko et al. (2026) learn a temperature so that α-entmax attention interpolates between sparse and dense regimes as the context grows. Our results transfer this observation to routing, where the score scale learned by the router, together with the map’s threshold, sets participation. This suggests that learned scales or shapes are the right knob for adaptive MoE routing. 

# 6 Conclusions 

We studied whether the sparsity of a probability map survives training in a top-2 MoE router. Across matched 300M and 1B models, the answer is that it survives only partly, and never as a property of the map alone. The 1.5-entmax router retains more probability mass than softmax while keeping both selected experts, the sparsemax router retains the most mass, and the normmax-2 router uses the fewest contributors. These behaviors are set jointly by the map’s sparsification threshold and by the score distribution the router learns under it. The 1.5-entmax router keeps both experts because its learned scores have half the spread of softmax’s, which keeps its top-2 gaps below the threshold, whereas sparsemax and normmax-2, which sparsify at the same gap, differ only because their routers learned different gap distributions. Retention, participation and predictive quality also turn out to be three distinct quantities: none of the sparse maps improves validation loss over softmax, yet all of them make the trained model less sensitive than softmax to increasing the inference-time budget. Our results also say where a sparse router should be designed, rather than merely swapped in. Because the router co-adapts its score scale to the map, that scale is a modelling choice that we currently leave to the optimizer, and it would be better learned explicitly, as is already done for sparse attention (Correia et al., 2019; Vasylenko et al., 2026). A router whose scale is under control could then be trusted to choose its own budget: the sparse maps already assign zero weight to most of the extra slots when K is raised, so selecting every expert in the support instead of a fixed top-K,capped by an at-most-k operator, is a small step from what the trained models already do. The balancing objective is another design choice to test: its gradients depend on the map, so changing the probabilities used for balancing may alter the score scales and expert loads learned during training. We leave these directions to future work. 

Limitations. Our matched comparison uses a single training seed per configuration, so the small quality differences between maps (at most 0.012 nats of validation loss) do not establish a ranking. We test two members of the entmax family ( α = 1 .5 and sparsemax, α = 2 ), one normmax ( γ = 2 ) and one balancing objective, whose probabilities we vary in a single 300M ablation. Contributor counts measure expert participation, not compute. As such, whether zero-weight experts are skipped, and what that saves, depends on the dispatch implementation, which we do not measure. We did not verify that the diagnostic samples are disjoint from the training data. Finally, the score-scale analysis is descriptive: it relates each router’s singleton rate to its learned gap distribution but does not identify the training mechanism that produced the smaller scales.

Pr( ρ g ≥ t). Because the router weights are trained by ordinary backpropagation under the map, nothing pins the scale, which is why §4.3 treats it as a learned quantity.
