## ABSTRACT 

Accurate bioactivity prediction is a central challenge in early-stage drug discovery, as individual assays often contain too few measurements to train reliable models independently. Meta-learning offers a principled approach to this few-shot setting, but assay heterogeneity may limit its effectiveness. Here, we test this hypothesis and show that meta-learning performance degrades as meta-training tasks become more heterogeneous. To address this, we introduce MetaHeta , a meta-learning framework that accounts for assay heterogeneity by conditioning predictions on auxiliary data from related assays, with relatedness defined flexibly from available assay information. The architecture of MetaHeta combines linear attention over large auxiliary datasets with exact attention over scarce task-specific context, en-abling efficient scaling to the former without compromising exact attention over the latter. We demonstrate the benefits of our approach on assays from ChEMBL and BindingDB, improving few-shot bioactivity prediction and downstream com-pound prioritization in retrospective Bayesian optimization. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Accurately predicting compound activity in biological assays is a central challenge in early-stage drug discovery. Reliable in silico predictors can guide candidate selection and therefore reduce the time and resources required for experimental evaluation (Jim´ enez-Luna et al., 2021; Scalia et al., 2026). Machine learning models have been widely employed for this task, but their performance typically relies on the availability of large amounts of high-quality data (Tian et al., 2024; Mayr et al., 2018). However, since bioactivity measurements are expensive to acquire, individual assays often contain too little data to train accurate models in isolation, placing assay-specific bioactivity prediction in a few-shot learning regime (Vella & Ebejer, 2022; Altae-Tran et al., 2017). A natural way to alleviate this data scarcity is to share information across assays, but naively pooling their measurements risks conflating distinct assay-specific relationships. A particularly promising approach to this problem is meta-learning (Schmidhuber, 1987; Hochreiter et al., 2001), which aims to learn shared structure across a collection of related tasks to facilitate adaptation to new ones, but without directly pooling their measurements. This collection of tasks is commonly referred to as the meta-dataset . In bioactivity prediction, each such task can natu-rally correspond to an individual assay, with a small number of observed compound–activity pairs forming a context set from which activities are predicted for new compounds ( target inputs ). A variety of meta-learning approaches have been developed to enable information sharing across tasks. Finn et al. (2017) introduced MAML, a gradient-based algorithm that learns a shared parameter initialization from which the model can adapt to new tasks in a few gradient steps. With a similar bi-level optimization objective, Chen et al. A heterogeneous meta-dataset can induce a broad prior, such that limited task-specific data are insufficient to concentrate the posterior around functions relevant to a new assay. Neural Processes (NPs) (Garnelo et al., 2018b;a) instead amortize task adaptation by learning a mapping from a set of con-text points to predictive distributions at tar-get locations, offering a scalable alterna-tive to GPs. Transformer Neural Processes (TNPs) (Nguyen & Grover, 2022) param-eterize this mapping using attention mech-anisms, making them well suited to mod-eling sets while capturing rich interactions between context and target points. This ar-chitecture also underpins Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs), which pre-train TNPs on massive synthetic datasets for off-the-shelf prediction on new tasks (M¨ uller et al., 2022). A key assumption underlying meta-learning is that tasks in the meta-dataset are sufficiently related to induce a shared prior that supports efficient adaptation to new tasks from limited data (Baxter, 2000). However, the heterogeneity of assays in large-scale bioactivity datasets, arising from differences in experimental conditions, endpoints, and measurement protocols (Landrum & Riniker, 2024), can render this assumption questionable, potentially limiting the effectiveness of meta-learning. We il-lustrate the intuition behind this potential failure mode in Figure 1. Indeed, prior work suggests that meta-learning methods struggle to adapt to task-specific bioactivity data (Stanley et al., 2021), with Schimunek et al. (2023) reporting that even a naive baseline that ignores the context set can outper-form several established meta-learning approaches. Here, we hypothesize that assay heterogeneity is a key factor limiting the effectiveness of meta-learning for bioactivity prediction, and explicitly account for it in a dedicated modeling framework. 

Our contributions are as follows: (i) we systematically study the effect of meta-training task heterogeneity on few-shot bioactivity prediction, demonstrating in a controlled setting that increasing heterogeneity can degrade meta-learning performance across a range of methods and context sizes; 

(ii) motivated by these findings, we introduce MetaHeta , a meta-learning framework in which predictions are additionally conditioned on data from related assays. This allows the model to approximate the posterior predictive under a more informative prior relevant to the target task, rather than under that induced by the entire heterogeneous meta-training distribution. Assay relatedness can be defined flexibly based on target homology or known experimental conditions; 

(iii) to make conditioning on such auxiliary data computationally practical, we develop a TNP-based architecture that combines exact attention over scarce task-specific context with linear attention over potentially large auxiliary datasets, enabling efficient conditioning at scale. We further demonstrate that findings from our controlled heterogeneity study translate into practical gains on assays from real-world bioactivity databases, improving few-shot bioactivity prediction and retrospective Bayesian optimization. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Early meta-learning approaches include metric-based methods (Vinyals et al., 2016; Snell et al., 2017), developed primarily for few-shot classification, and adaptation-based methods that learn shared structure across tasks to enable rapid task-specific adaptation from limited data (Finn et al., 2017; Nichol et al., 2018). These ideas have also been applied to few-shot molecular property predic-tion (Nguyen et al., 2020; Altae-Tran et al., 2017; Schimunek et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021; 2024), with much of this work focusing on classification. More directly relevant to our probabilistic regres-sion setting, deep kernel-based methods meta-learn shared feature representations while retaining task-specific Gaussian process inference (Chen et al., 2023; Patacchiola et al., 2020). A comple-mentary line of work instead amortizes task adaptation by directly learning a mapping from task-specific context and target inputs to predictive distributions, with Neural Processes (NPs) forming a canonical family of such methods (Garnelo et al., 2018b;a; Kim et al., 2019; Rochussen & Fortuin, 2026). In recent years, NPs equipped with transformer architectures (Vaswani et al., 2017) (TNPs) 2Preprint have emerged as a prominent variant (Nguyen & Grover, 2022; Feng et al., 2023; Ashman, 2024), with Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) further extending this paradigm by pre-training TNPs on broad synthetic task distributions (M¨ uller et al., 2022). This line of work has been particularly influ-ential in tabular learning (Hollmann et al., 2025; Grinsztajn et al., 2026; Qu et al., 2026), with recent studies showing that tabular foundation models can exhibit capabilities beyond conventional tabular settings (Nakerst et al., 2026), including molecular property prediction (Hicham et al., 2026). We also discuss methods that explicitly account for task heterogeneity in Appendix D.

A shared input MLP embeds context, target, and concatenated auxiliary observations, augmented with task-of-origin indicators, with target activities masked to 0. Encoder layers use exact self-attention for the context and FAVOR+ for auxiliary data, followed by context-to-auxiliary and target-to-context cross-attention. Our architecture, shown in Figure 2, addresses this using two coupled TNP-like branches that share attention parameters while employing different self-attention mechanisms. For both branches, each observation [x, y, e j ] is first embedded with an MLP, with target observations having their activities masked to 0. Here, ej is a one-hot indicator of the task of origin, allowing observations from all auxiliary datasets to be concatenated into a single sequence without ambiguity. To preserve invariance to permutations of auxiliary datasets, the indicators are assigned using a canonical ordering of Daux , for example by task relatedness (Proposition 2 in Ap-pendix G). Within each encoder layer, context representations attend to the auxiliary representations, before target representations attend to the augmented context. For the context branch, we employ standard softmax self-attention, whereas the auxiliary branch uses FAVOR+ (Choromanski et al., 2021), a linear-time approximation based on positive orthogonal random features. Since FAVOR+ provides an unbiased estimator of the softmax kernel, the same parameters can be used under both attention schemes while still targeting the same underlying operation. This allows us to scale ef-ficiently to large Daux without sacrificing exact attention over the task-specific context. The use of coupled TNP-like branches builds on ICICL-TNP (Ashman et al., 2024); we provide a detailed comparison of the two architectures in Appendix D. 

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We structure the experiments as follows. In Section 5.1, we investigate the effect of meta-training task heterogeneity on meta-learning performance in a controlled environment. In Section 5.2, we show the few-shot bioactivity prediction capabilities of MetaHeta on real-world bioactivity assays from ChEMBL (Gaulton et al., 2012). In Section 5.3, we demonstrate its effectiveness for down-stream compound prioritization through retrospective Bayesian optimization. Finally, in Section 5.4, we ablate the auxiliary-conditioning mechanism and examine its computational cost. All results are averaged over five random seeds and reported with standard errors. 5.1 META -LEARNING UNDER CONTROLLED ASSAY HETEROGENEITY 

To study the effect of task heterogeneity in isolation, we construct a semi-synthetic benchmark based on kinase activity screening data from Drewry et al. (2017). Kinases provide a well-suited testbed: their common ATP-binding function is reflected in a highly conserved catalytic domain, while in-hibitor response depends largely on variation at a small subset of active-site residues (Sheridan et al., 2009; Born et al., 2022). Coupled with the well-defined phylogenetic organization of the kinome, these properties provide a natural, biologically meaningful hierarchy of task relatedness.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we first investigate the effect of meta-training task heterogeneity on few-shot bioac-tivity prediction in a controlled setting, and show that increasing heterogeneity can systematically 9Preprint degrade meta-learning performance across methods and context sizes. To address this, we intro-duce MetaHeta , a meta-learning framework which explicitly accounts for assay heterogeneity by conditioning predictions on auxiliary data from related assays. Its architecture enables this auxil-iary conditioning to scale efficiently to large datasets while preserving exact attention over scarce task-specific context. We further demonstrate the effectiveness of our method under three notions of assay relatedness: shared target identity, phylogenetic relationships between targets, and assay-description similarity. Finally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of MetaHeta on bioactivity assays from ChEMBL and BindingDB, improving both few-shot prediction and downstream compound pri-oritization through retrospective Bayesian optimization. We discuss the limitations of our approach and directions for future work in Appendix E. Taken together, our results identify assay heterogene-ity as an important challenge for few-shot bioactivity meta-learning and show that conditioning on related assays provides an effective and scalable way to address it. ## A EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

A.1 CONTROLLED -HETEROGENEITY BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION 

Data preparation We construct the semi-synthetic kinase benchmark from the PKIS2 profiling data of Drewry et al. (2017), comprising 645 compounds from 86 chemotypes screened against 392 wild-type human kinases. We first restrict the panel to the kinases considered in our benchmark and preprocess the molecular structures with RDKit (Landrum et al., 2026) by selecting the parent fragment, neutralizing charges, and canonicalizing tautomers. Activity values are transformed from the unit interval to logit space after clipping extreme values. Molecules are represented using 1024-bit Morgan fingerprints with radius 2. 

Kinase-specific GP fitting For each kinase, we standardize the transformed activity values and fit an exact Gaussian process with a constant mean and a scaled Tanimoto kernel over Morgan fingerprints. This yields a separate posterior distribution over activity functions for each kinase. We assess each kinase-specific GP using leave-one-chemotype-out cross-validation. In each fold, one chemotype is held out, the GP is refitted on the remaining compounds, and predictive performance is compared against a baseline that predicts the mean activity of the training fold. We retain only kinases with positive balanced leave-one-chemotype-out. 

Molecule generation To obtain a sufficiently large molecular domain for synthetic task genera-tion, we expand the original kinase-panel chemistry using CReM (Polishchuk, 2020) mutation and growth operations. Generated molecules are filtered to satisfy basic drug-like property constraints, requiring molecular weight between 150 and 650 , log P between −1 and 7, topological polar sur-face area below 180 , at most 6 hydrogen-bond donors, 12 hydrogen-bond acceptors, and 15 rotatable bonds. We further retain only molecules whose maximum Tanimoto similarity to any molecule in the original panel lies between 0.40 and 0.60 , yielding approximately 10 6 candidate molecules. These molecules are then partitioned into training, validation, and test molecular domains using an 

80 /10 /10 Bemis–Murcko scaffold split, with scaffolds kept disjoint across the three partitions. 

Task construction Each synthetic assay is generated by first sampling its size from a PERT dis-tribution with minimum 40 , mode 100 , maximum 1024 , and concentration 20 . We then sample the corresponding number of molecules from the relevant molecular split and select a kinase-specific GP. Activities are generated by drawing jointly from the GP posterior over the selected molecules and adding Gaussian observation noise, with the fitted observation-noise standard deviation scaled by 0.5. Using this procedure, we first generate a core meta-dataset of 5000 tasks from GPs cor-responding to EPHA kinases. We then generate an additional 5000 tasks for each heterogeneity regime while keeping the sampled task sizes and molecular inputs fixed across regimes. Only the kinase-specific GP used to generate the activity values is varied, with source GPs corresponding to (i) EPHA kinases, (ii) non-EPHA tyrosine kinases, or (iii) non-tyrosine kinases. We additionally generate 250 validation and 250 test assays from EPHA kinase GPs. For the homologous-target transfer experiment, we generate 250 validation and 250 test assays from EPHB kinase GPs, while keeping training restricted to EPHA tasks.

Data preparation We curate assays from BindingDB (Liu et al., 2025) for retrospective Bayesian optimization experiments. Molecular structures are first processed with RDKit (Landrum et al., 2026) using the same procedure as described in Appendix A.1. We restrict the data to measure-ments against human, single-chain protein targets and remove target names associated with more than one UniProt (Consortium, 2025) identifier or more than one target-chain sequence, ensuring an unambiguous mapping between target name and protein identity. We further retain only BindingDB entries for which all measurements associated with a given DOI correspond to a single target. We consider affinity measurements reported as Ki, IC 50 , or Kd, and retain only observations for which exactly one of these three endpoints is available, discarding observations with either no usable mea-surement or multiple reported endpoints. The remaining measurements are converted to a common 

p-activity scale as Y = 9 − log 10 (x), where x is the reported affinity in nM. Finally, compounds with molecular weight above 900 Da are removed. 

Assay selection We select assays that are sufficiently large and chemically diverse to sup-port retrospective compound optimization while retaining a non-degenerate activity landscape. Specifically, we require each assay to contain between 500 and 2000 compounds and at least 30 Bemis–Murcko scaffolds (Bemis & Murcko, 1996) represented by at least five compounds each. We further exclude assays for which any single activity value accounts for more than 

25% of the measurements. Finally, since the optimization experiments are commonly initial-ized from a single weakly active chemical series, we require each assay to contain at least five eligible starting scaffolds, defined as Bemis–Murcko scaffolds represented by at least five compounds whose median activity lies at or below the assay-specific 25th percentile. This ensures that each retained assay admits multiple chemically distinct low-activity starting series from which to simulate early-stage compound optimization. Since BindingDB contains records originating from both ChEMBL and the patent literature, we restrict assay selection to those derived from the latter to avoid potential overlap with the ChEMBL-based FS-Mol training data. We evaluate all methods at context sizes |D C | ∈ { 4, 8, 16 , 32 }. We largely follow Stanley et al. (2021), but deviate in how context size is handled during training and validation. Specifically, for each context size, we train and validate a separate model using that same fixed context size, and evaluate it only at the corresponding context size. Given the highly data-scarce regime considered here, this ensures that every method is optimized for the same task-specific data budget on which it is evaluated, and avoids conflating few-shot performance with generalization across context sizes. This also places amortized methods, which can naturally accommodate varying context sizes, on the same footing as prior FS-Mol evaluations in which methods are trained using a fixed support-set size (Stanley et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023). For each evaluation assay, we sample 10 context– target splits and average evaluation metrics across splits before averaging across assays. For the controlled-heterogeneity experiments, all methods are given the same molecular representation used by the task-generating GPs, namely 1024-bit radius-2 Morgan fingerprints. This ensures that dif-ferences in performance relative to the GP oracle cannot be attributed to a mismatch in molecular 19 Preprint representation. For the experiments on FS-Mol, we do not enforce a single representation across methods: we align representations where appropriate, while retaining method-specific feature ex-tractors when they are part of the original approach or using alternative representations when these perform better. This is because imposing a single shared representation may disadvantage partic-ular baselines whose performance is strongly tied to the choice of molecular representation. For the retrospective Bayesian optimization experiments, we train models with the context size sampled uniformly from {4, . . . , 32 }. During validation, we evaluate each model on three independently sampled context–target splits at each context size |D C | ∈ { 4, 8, 16 , 32 }, and average performance across all splits and context sizes. Controlled-heterogeneity experiment Our model is trained with meta-batch size 32 for 10

FS-Mol experiment For FS-Mol, we use a meta-batch size of 16 and train for 100 epochs with learning rate 3 × 10 −4. We evaluate on the validation set after each epoch and retain the checkpoint with the lowest validation NLL. The architecture is increased to an embedding and feed-forward dimension of 256 , decoder dimension 128 , 8 attention layers, and head dimension 32 , while retaining

Auxiliary dataset construction The construction of Daux in MetaHeta is adapted to the notion of assay relatedness available in each experimental setting: • Controlled-heterogeneity experiment. We construct Daux according to Eq. 5, selecting at most five same-target datasets, ordered by decreasing average 5-nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity to the current task. The similarity is computed exclusively from molecular inputs; target outputs are never used in auxiliary-dataset selection. Auxiliary datasets containing more than 512 observations are randomly subsampled to that size. • Homologous-target transfer experiment. For the EPHB evaluation, where same-target aux-iliary assays are unavailable, we instead construct Daux according to Proposition 1. For each such EPHB assay, we sample five auxiliary tasks from the pool of assays targeting any member of the EPHA subfamily and order them by decreasing activity variance. Each auxiliary dataset is again subsampled to at most 512 observations. • FS-Mol & retrospective Bayesian optimization. We construct Daux using assay-description similarity, as described in Algorithm 1, retaining at most five auxiliary datasets per task and subsampling each to at most 512 observations. A.4.2 BASELINES 

Implementations of the baseline methods were based primarily on the code released by Chen et al. (2023), Stanley et al. (2021), and implementations of several Neural Process variants available at 

https://github.com/cambridge-mlg/tnp , released under the MIT License. • Transformer Neural Process (TNP) (Nguyen & Grover, 2022). We use the same archi-tecture and training setup as for our model. In particular, setting the number of auxiliary datasets to zero reduces our model to the standard TNP-D (using the efficient implementa-tion described by Feng et al. (2023)). • Conditional Neural Process (CNP) (Garnelo et al., 2018a). For FS-Mol, we use mean ag-gregation and a 256 -dimensional context representation, with a decoder hidden dimension 20 Preprint of 256 and with Hyformer embeddings as molecular representations. For the controlled-heterogeneity experiment we reduce both the context representation and decoder hidden dimensions to 128 .• Attentive Neural Process (ANP) (Kim et al., 2019). We match the TNP baseline in ar-chitectural parameters, including the embedding, feed-forward, decoder, and attention di-mensions, as well as the number of attention heads and layers, while omitting the latent path. • Adaptive Deep Kernel Fitting with Implicit Function Theorem (ADKF-IFT) (Chen et al., 2023). For FS-Mol, we retain the feature extractor used in the original implementa-tion, combining ECFP fingerprints with a GNN-MT from Stanley et al. (2021). For the controlled-heterogeneity experiment, we replace the GNN with a two-layer MLP with hid-den and output dimensions of 1024 , operating on the same 1024 -bit fingerprints used by the GP oracle. • Deep Kernel Transfer (DKT) (Patacchiola et al., 2020). We use the implementation pro-vided by Chen et al. (2023) and follow the same experimental setup and feature-extractor choices as for ADKF-IFT. • Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) (Finn et al., 2017). We use the implementation from Stanley et al. (2021) based on GNN-ST. For the controlled-heterogeneity experiment, we replace the GNN with the same two-layer MLP used for ADKF-IFT and DKT. The regression head for the kinase experiments is an MLP with hidden dimensions 256 and 64 .• ST-MAML (Wang et al., 2022). We preserve the MAML setup, adding a stochastic task encoder with hidden dimension 128, a 32-dimensional latent representation, and a 32-dimensional task feature. We weight the KL regularization term by 0.1 and average predic-tions over five context-conditioned latent samples at evaluation. • Frequent Hitters (Schimunek et al., 2023). Frequent Hitters is simply an MLP trained on data pooled across all training assays, without using the support set of the target assay. Following a hyperparameter sweep, we use four hidden layers of width 16 , batch size 

256 , learning rate 10 −3, no dropout or weight decay, and train for up to 100 epochs with an early-stopping patience of 10 epochs. Molecular inputs are represented using ECFP fingerprints. • Similarity Search. As an additional sanity-check baseline proposed by Schimunek et al. (2023), we use Morgan fingerprints with radius 2 and 1024 bits, and predict each target as the Tanimoto-similarity-weighted average of the context labels. • TabPFNv3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026). We use Morgan fingerprints with radius 2 and 1024 

bits as molecular representations, which we found to perform better than Hyformer embed-dings. • TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026). We use the same molecular representation as for TabPFNv3. • ICICL-TNP (Ashman et al., 2024). Across all settings, we always construct Daux in the same way as for MetaHeta , such that both models condition on exactly the same auxiliary data. As in MetaHeta , we share the projection parameters between the corresponding MHSA and MHCA blocks of the context and auxiliary branches. We do not include dataset-of-origin one-hot indicators, as auxiliary datasets are represented separately through their pseudo-token sets. We use 64 pseudo-tokens for each auxiliary dataset and set the number of context pseudo-tokens equal to the context size, |D C |.• RF-Daux . We construct Daux in the same way as for MetaHeta and then concatenate its datasets with the task-specific context set. A random forest with 250 trees is then fit to that combined dataset. ## D EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Task heterogeneity in meta-learning Task heterogeneity in meta-learning has been addressed by prior work through two broad strategies. One line of work explicitly models multiple task regimes using mixtures of meta-learners or hierarchical task structures, with individual components spe-cializing to subsets of related tasks (Wang & van Hoof, 2022; Yao et al., 2019; Jerfel et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2021; Chan et al., 2023). These approaches typically rely on a prespecified number of task clusters or mixture components, thereby imposing some prior structure on the task distribution. This is difficult to justify for bioactivity prediction, where assay heterogeneity arises from multiple interacting factors and may not admit a small, disjoint set of task regimes. In large assay collec-tions the relevant regimes may be numerous and overlapping, making a global mixture cardinality difficult to specify. Among these approaches, Jerfel et al. (2019) is an exception, using a Dirichlet process mixture that can introduce additional components as it encounters new task regimes, but still assumes a discrete mixture structure over tasks. A complementary line of work, more directly com-parable to our setting, instead infers task-specific representations from the available context set and uses them to condition or modulate the meta-learner, thereby adapting to heterogeneous task distri-butions without explicitly enumerating the underlying task regimes (Wang et al., 2022; Vettoruzzo et al., 2024; Vuorio et al., 2019). Notably, Wang et al. (2022) explicitly address the difficulty of inferring task identity from scarce context by representing it as a stochastic latent variable, which captures uncertainty over the underlying task regime. 

Comparison with ICICL-TNP (Ashman et al., 2024) The architecture of MetaHeta is re-lated to ICICL-TNP, but differs primarily in how information from multiple auxiliary datasets is represented and exchanged. Both architectures use coupled TNP-style processing paths for the task-specific context and auxiliary data, while avoiding quadratic attention over the potentially large auxiliary datasets. ICICL-TNP achieves this using a Perceiver-style (Jaegle et al., 2021) pseudo-token architecture: (i) the task-specific context set and each auxiliary dataset are separately en-23 Preprint coded into their own sets of pseudo-tokens; (ii) the auxiliary pseudo-tokens attend to the context pseudo-tokens; (iii) self-attention is applied separately within each set of pseudo-tokens; and (iv) the context pseudo-tokens attend back to the auxiliary pseudo-tokens before conditioning the target representations. Consequently, observations from different auxiliary datasets exchange information only indirectly. MetaHeta concatenates auxiliary observations into a single sequence and applies linear self-attention across them, enabling direct information exchange between observations from different auxiliary datasets. This is made possible by augmenting each observation with a one-hot indicator of its task of origin, which preserves task identity after concatenation, while the use of FAVOR+ (Choromanski et al., 2021) in the auxiliary branch keeps self-attention computationally efficient even for large auxiliary sets. This removes the pseudo-token bottleneck and, in our setting, enables substantially more efficient conditioning on large auxiliary collections. However, as a result of representing task identity explicitly through one-hot indicators, the input embedding dimension fixes the maximum number of auxiliary datasets that can be accommodated without modifying the architecture. ICICL-TNP does not have this restriction, since task identity is represented structurally through separate pseudo-token sets. 

## E DISCUSSION 

E.1 LIMITATIONS 

Scope of the theoretical guarantees Both the result of Ashman et al. (2024) in Eq. 5 and Propo-sition 1, which motivate our selection of auxiliary datasets, are derived for exact Bayesian posterior predictive distributions under their respective generative assumptions. They therefore do not di-rectly guarantee improvements for finite-capacity models trained on a finite meta-dataset. Likewise, description-based relatedness is only a heuristic for identifying useful auxiliary assays. However, we demonstrate that auxiliary conditioning remains effective across all three settings considered in this work: when relatedness is defined by shared target identity, by homology between distinct targets, and heuristically from assay descriptions. In the latter setting, randomly shuffling the re-trieved auxiliary assays (Section 5.4) substantially degrades predictive performance, indicating that the observed gains depend on meaningful assay relatedness. 

Fixed auxiliary-dataset capacity Because MetaHeta encodes dataset identity using one-hot indicators, the maximum number of auxiliary datasets is fixed by the input dimensionality and cannot be increased after training without modifying the model, as elaborated in Appendix D. In practice, however, we found this limitation to be mild: the model can simply be trained with a conservative upper bound and evaluated with fewer auxiliary datasets. Our ablation in Section 5.4 demonstrates that performance remains strong across varying inference-time auxiliary-set cardinalities. E.2 FUTURE WORK 

Proposition 1 considers a two-level hierarchy in which protein-specific components share a common family-level component. In practice, biological relatedness can exhibit richer structure. A natural extension would therefore be to incorporate multiple levels of relatedness, allowing auxiliary in-formation to be shared across increasingly broad groups of related targets. Beyond hierarchical structure, future work could also incorporate additional data modalities, such as protein sequence or structure and richer assay metadata, to define relatedness more comprehensively.
