ABSTRACT
Intermediate-layer features from multimodal large language models have shown
strong potential for video anomaly detection (VAD), yet the origin of their dis-
criminative power remains unclear. We study this question using sparse mixture-
of-experts (MoE) models, whose explicit expert structure and sparse activation
make their internal computation easier to inspect. With a fully frozen backbone
and no additional training, we find that anomaly-related evidence is concentrated
in a small set of experts. These experts recur across layers, spontaneously spe-
cialize in different anomaly types, and together form a dynamic routing subnet-
work. We further show that the output channels most strongly influenced by these
experts are also the hidden dimensions that contain the most anomaly-relevant in-
formation. Routing statistics can therefore serve as an internal anomaly cue that
complements semantic features. Based on these findings, we propose RoMod, an
efficient VAD framework trained with only 5% of weakly labeled videos. RoMod
includes a Routing-Modulated Fusion module, RoMF, and a Routing-aware Tem-
poral Network, RoTN. RoMF uses routing signals to adaptively recalibrate hidden
semantic channels. Its design also prevents the routing branch from bypassing se-
mantic features and making predictions on its own. RoTN captures the temporal
evolution of anomalies from onset to persistence and termination. Experiments on
three benchmarks show that RoMod achieves state-of-the-art performance while
running substantially faster than dense backbones of comparable size.

INTRODUCTION
Latent representation capacity.
The internal representations of large pre-trained models are often
more useful than their generated answers, and across discriminative tasks a lightweight probe on a
frozen backbone routinely outperforms the model’s own language output [1, 3, 5, 4, 6]. Dense
Transformers, however, activate every parameter for every input, so semantic content, scene layout
and capture conditions all end up entangled in the same weights. That makes it hard to say where
discriminability emerges, which units carry it, or how it spreads across channels.
Video anomaly detection as a testbed.
VAD sharpens all three questions. Training labels exist
only at the video level while evaluation demands precise temporal localization, so a detector has to
separate normal from anomalous clips using its internal representations alone. Violence, accidents
and disasters also look nothing alike, and the only thing tying them together is their departure from
normal behaviour. In a surveillance stream both accuracy and latency matter, which is where sparse
MoE backbones help. Their gating paths split a dense layer into separate, inspectable experts, and
only a small subset of those experts fires for any given input [26, 16, 12].

The RoMod framework.
Hidden states describe visual content, whereas routing records which
computational pathway the model chose. We combine the two by modulation rather than concate-
nation, letting the routing vector produce a scale for every hidden channel so that anomaly-related
channels are enhanced and background noise damped, without ever replacing the visual-semantic
signal. RoMod implements this idea with RoMF and RoTN. Rather than fixing a layer or an expert
index in advance, it uses clip-level pseudo-labels from 5% of the weakly labelled videos to select a
layer, learn the channel modulation and train the temporal model.
Contributions.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows.
• A mechanistic analysis. A forward-only, training-free study of an MoE backbone locates
where discriminability arises, identifies the small expert group that carries anomaly seman-
tics, and shows how routing shifts with anomaly type even without category supervision.
Expert-channel co-activation statistics tie routing, channel updates and discriminability into
one chain.
• Routing-modulated fusion. RoMF recalibrates hidden channels through a gating-driven
multiplicative residual that keeps the semantic and routing branches distinct, while RoTN
models the onset, development and end of anomalous states. Together they give a concrete
way to exploit internal routing signals from a frozen sparse model.
• A low-resource framework. A single set of pseudo-labels guides layer selection, modu-
lation and temporal supervision, so neither frame-level labels nor an oracle layer choice is
required. Within a 5% data budget, RoMod reaches state-of-the-art results on three bench-
marks and supports low-latency streaming detection.

RELATED WORK
Weakly supervised video anomaly detection.
Frame-level annotation is infeasible for long
surveillance footage, so mainstream approaches pair video-level weak labels with multiple-instance
learning. A video becomes a bag and a clip an instance, and bag-level supervision is pushed down
through ranking losses or top-k aggregation, usually together with temporal smoothing, magnitude
constraints or uncertainty modelling to suppress the many normal clips sitting inside anomalous
bags [27, 28, 34, 30]. The representation source has migrated over time, from two-stream action
features such as C3D and I3D [29, 9], to vision-language embeddings such as CLIP [24], and more
recently to intermediate-layer hidden states of multimodal large language models [6, 33, 19]. That

last option brings sizeable gains, yet it is used in a strictly static way. One picks a layer, extracts
the hidden state and attaches a temporal head, while the question of which sub-structures the model
activated, and with what weights, is discarded entirely.
Expert specialization and internal signals in MoE models.
Sparse MoE architectures now sup-
port a whole generation of large language and multimodal models at activation costs far below dense
models of equivalent capacity [26, 16, 12], and their gating mechanism naturally raises the ques-
tion of whether experts differentiate functionally. Existing analyses concentrate on pure language
models, reporting expert preferences for lexical categories, languages or syntactic roles along with
regularities in how routing evolves with depth, and their conclusions serve load balancing, expert
pruning or routing stability [35]. Treating the routing signal itself as a representation for down-
stream use, particularly for visual temporal discrimination, has to our knowledge not been studied
systematically. We neither modify the backbone nor retrain the gate. On a fully frozen model we
simply read routing as an internal record of decisions and use it to modulate hidden representations.

3.1
WHERE DISCRIMINABILITY LIVES AND WHICH UNITS CARRY IT
Feature evolution exhibits three stages.
For each layer we measure Fisher separability between
normal and anomalous hidden states, comparing the distance between the two class means against
the variation inside each class. From L1 to L17 the score sits near zero, because at these depths the
representation still describes appearance, texture and local motion, leaving normal and anomalous
clips mixed together. It then rises sharply around L18–L27, peaks at L28, and stays high past the
plateau. Anomaly semantics thus emerge within a limited depth range instead of accumulating
evenly across the network, and once they appear they remain a stable property of the middle-to-
late feature space rather than an accident of one layer. Any layer on this plateau is a reasonable
candidate, which is what motivates the adaptive selection scheme in Section 4.1. Appendix C gives
the setup, the metric derivation and the full curves.
Anomaly semantics concentrate on very few experts.
Separability tells us useful information
exists but not which experts carry it. For each layer–expert pair we count how often that expert has a
non-zero activation in a clip and use this single number as the anomaly score, with no hidden feature,
temporal context or trainable parameter involved. On XD-Violence, expert L26-E199 reaches 91.6%
ROC-AUC and 77.40% AP from activation counts alone, far above the random AP baseline of
24.24% and recovering 93% of the AP of the hidden-state probe (Figure 1a). Transferring the top
20 XD-Violence experts unchanged, the best one still reaches 83.7% ROC-AUC on UCF-Crime
and 71.2% AUC on UBnormal, so these experts capture a transferable notion of abnormality rather
than a dataset-specific scene bias. Performance declines gradually across the top 20, suggesting the
signal is spread over a small expert group instead of residing in one indispensable unit.
Shallow high-response experts reveal a signal misalignment.
Some high-response experts sit
in shallow layers such as L07, L08 and L10, and this does not contradict the poor separability of
shallow hidden states. Low-level irregularities can already sway the gate even when the hidden
state has not yet expressed that information in a linearly separable form, and it is the feed-forward
transforms of later layers that write the discriminative signal into the hidden space. Exactly this
mismatch is why routing deserves treatment as a separate feature source.

The causal chain closes at the level of hidden channels.
A standardized channel discriminability
analysis locates the hidden dimensions that separate normal and abnormal clips most inside the high
plateau. By quantifying the co-activation covariance between routing decisions and residual updates,
we find that these discriminative channels align precisely with the hidden dimensions driven most
strongly by the high-response anomaly experts, and zeroing those experts in a causal knockout sub-
stantially impairs the model’s anomaly readout (derivations and knockout results are in Appendix B).
The chain therefore closes. An anomalous visual cue drives the gate towards anomaly-specialized
experts, whose feed-forward transforms selectively update specific channels and so endow the hid-
den state with strong linear discriminability. The layer-wise emergence of separability originates in
the routing of this sparse specialized group and their coordinated channel writes, and the normal-
versus-anomalous contrast in Figure 1b is its direct manifestation at the gating level.
3.3
DESIGN CONSTRAINTS IMPLIED BY THE DIAGNOSIS
Asymmetry between the two signals dictates the fusion operator.
Hidden states carry the se-
mantic content of scenes, objects and behaviours and form the substrate of discrimination, while
routing is an input-dependent selector over computational pathways. Concatenation or summation
would erase this asymmetry and let the model bypass semantics to fit the scene directly. We in-
stead let routing generate per-channel scaling factors that strengthen anomaly-bound channels and
suppress background interference through a multiplicative residual (Section 4.2). Because expert
parameters are independent across layers and effective experts are scattered throughout the network,
the modulation module hard-codes no expert index and takes the complete routing vector of the se-
lected layer as input, learning the expert-to-channel mapping from data. The width of the plateau
also means that a small amount of weak supervision is enough to locate a layer balancing discrim-
inability against cost, with no oracle layer selection needed (Section 4.1).

4
METHOD: ROMOD
Section 3 motivates the three core ingredients of RoMod: an adaptive layer-selection criterion guided
by the Fisher plateau, a global routing fingerprint that captures the sparse sub-network, and a gating-
modulated fusion operator aligned with how experts write to channels. RoMod (Figure 3) never
touches test ground truth, keeps the multimodal backbone completely frozen, and works in two
stages on only 5% of the weakly labelled videos. Stage I induces clip-level pseudo-labels through
lightweight MIL, and those labels then act as the sole supervision in Stage II, where the channel
modulation and the causal temporal network are trained end-to-end.
4.1
DUAL-PATH SIGNAL READOUT AND ADAPTIVE LAYER SELECTION
Both signals come from a single forward pass.
We divide each surveillance video into T non-
overlapping clips of Tf = 24 frames, sampling K = 4 of them uniformly. Each clip goes into
an anomaly-query prompt P = (Pprefix, Psuffix) and then through the frozen MoE backbone, which
has L layers with E experts each and routes to the top Ke experts. One forward pass yields two
complementary signals for clip t. The first is the hidden state H(l)
clip(t) at candidate layer l, a D-
dimensional vector representing visual semantics. The second is the routing fingerprint Rclip(t),
which concatenates the normalized expert-activation distributions of all L layers. Since the gating
computation already produces this information, collecting the fingerprint costs almost nothing. For
our 40-layer, 256-expert model it holds 40 × 256 = 10,240 values and records exactly which sub-
networks the input selected.
Stage I: inducing pseudo-labels from a 5% weak-label budget.
We sample 5% of the weakly
labelled training videos to form D5%. Inside its anomalous portion, videos are drawn uniformly
over the annotated anomaly categories, so rare event types appear in proportion to the taxonomy
rather than to their raw frequency. Each selected video carries only a binary label Y , zero for normal
and one for anomalous. A normal video is a pure-negative bag whose every clip receives a pseudo-
label of zero directly, so the induction procedure only has to mine positives inside the anomalous
bags. The frozen language head first provides a zero-shot anomaly score SMLLM, which initializes
a lightweight category-balanced MIL scorer trained with the loss LMIL. Since l⋆is itself chosen
from the pseudo-labels produced here, the scorer reads a fixed reference layer lref = L instead of l⋆.
We then suppress false peaks with normal semantic anchors and smooth the result with a Gaussian
temporal filter, which gives a clip-level pseudo-label ˆyt between zero and one. After Stage I the
scorer is frozen and discarded, and its pseudo-labels become the only supervision used in Stage II.
Appendix D gives the full algorithm and its refinement dynamics.

4.3
CAUSAL TEMPORAL MODELLING AND STAGE-II TRAINING (ROTN)
The temporal network explicitly models the onset and end of anomalous states.
The mod-
ulated clip features {zt}T

5
EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We evaluate RoMod on three benchmarks. XD-Violence covers multi-scene violence detection and
is reported mainly as frame-level AP, UCF-Crime covers thirteen categories of real-world criminal
events and is reported as frame-level ROC-AUC, and UBnormal contains synthetic anomalies with
a clear appearance-domain gap from the other two, which makes it a useful probe of cross-domain
generalization, also reported as AUC. Training uses only 5% of the weakly labelled videos sampled
with category balancing on anomalies (Section 4.1), and all remaining training videos as well as all
test ground truth stay unavailable. The default backbone is a frozen Qwen3.6-35B-A3B [22] with
40 MoE layers and roughly 2.4B parameters activated per step, and backbone independence is ver-
ified on Qwen3VL-30B-A3B [2] and Gemma-4-26B-A4B [13]. A dense Qwen3.8-27B [23] serves
only as a cost reference in Section 5.4, since it emits no routing distribution. Trainable parameters
amount to the routing projection branch plus a two-layer causal temporal encoder, while clip du-
ration, prompt template, bottleneck width and loss weights are listed in Appendix F. The attention
mask of the temporal network decides the deployment mode. Online mode uses causal attention,
where each clip sees only history and KV-cached incremental inference is available, whereas of-
fline mode uses bidirectional attention and suits offline retrieval and post-hoc analysis. Main results
report both, ablations report offline, and the efficiency analysis reports online.
5.2
COMPARISON WITH EXISTING METHODS
RoMod in offline mode leads on all four reported metrics (Table 1). On XD-Violence it reaches
92.49% AP and 97.34% AUC, above LAVIDA’s previous best AP of 90.62% and UR-DMU’s AUC
of 94.02%. On UCF-Crime it reaches 91.32% AUC, higher than the 90.33% of the fully supervised
π-VAD, which is notable given that RoMod sees only 5% of the weakly labelled training videos. On
UBnormal it obtains 79.17% AUC and again exceeds the full-data Anom-π result of 78.75%. Since
UBnormal has a clear appearance gap from the other two datasets, this outcome supports the analysis
in Section 3.1, where the routing signal appears to capture abnormal behaviour patterns that transfer
beyond a particular scene distribution. Switching to online inference with causal attention costs
about 1.6, 2.4 and 2.0 percentage points on UBnormal, UCF-Crime and XD-Violence respectively,
which is expected once the model can no longer inspect future clips. Even so, the online variant
stays competitive with most full-data and zero-shot methods, so the accuracy of the approach does
not rest on offline temporal context alone.

LAYER-WISE GAINS AND QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS
Figures 4b and 4c break the plateau of Section 3.1 down layer by layer from L20 to L40. The Hidden-

troughing at L28 and L38, so a fixed layer choice is unreliable, whereas RoMod improves every
tested layer, staying above 0.90 on XD-Violence and close to 0.90 on UCF-Crime with its largest
gains where Hidden-only is weakest, over 0.35 at L31. Routing thus compensates for poorly sepa-
rated hidden states, which makes the pseudo-label-based layer selection of Section 4.1 more robust.
Figure 4a shows the same contrast: on the normal video Salt.2010 the baseline throws repeated false
alarms above the 0.5 threshold at shot cuts and illumination changes while RoMod stays near the
noise floor, confirming that mean-centring with multiplicative modulation suppresses scene-related
channels, and on the fight (Kill.Bill) and riot (Riot) videos RoMod reacts at onset, holds high scores
through the window and tracks the multi-segment ground truth, whereas the baseline dips inside
anomalous segments and rises inside normal ones. Appendix H presents extended visualizations
across diverse accident and crime categories.
6
CONCLUSION
This paper uses a sparse MoE backbone to ask why the intermediate representations of large models
work so well for VAD. Our training-free analysis finds anomaly evidence in a small MoE routing
sub-network whose experts specialize across layers and write to the hidden channels most useful
for discrimination, making routing statistics an internal decision signal that complements visual
semantics. RoMod builds on this with RoMF for routing-driven channel recalibration and RoTN
for causal temporal modelling, reaching state-of-the-art results on three benchmarks from 5% of
the weakly labelled videos while supporting low-latency streaming and suggesting that the internal
dynamics of sparse large models can inform the design of efficient downstream detectors.

NOTATION AND CONVENTIONS
The appendices reuse several letters across different roles. Table 5 fixes the convention followed

Layer indexing convention.
MoE decoder layers are indexed one-based throughout the paper,
the appendices and all figure axes: l = 1, . . . , L with L = 40, so the layers are L1 to L40 and
“Lk” always denotes the k-th MoE layer. Layer and residual-stream indices coincide under this
convention: hidden index 0 is the input embedding, and the update emitted by MoE layer l is first
observed at hidden index l, so the last layer L40 writes hidden index 40. Two layer ranges recur and
are deliberately distinct. The diagnostic band L20–L39 (20 layers) is the window over which the
routing-preference heatmaps (Section 3.2), the divergence tests (Appendix B.2) and the knockout
intervention (Appendix B.4) are computed; its round width keeps the layer averages in Eq. 5 and the
expert budgets of Appendix B.4 directly comparable. The candidate range L = {lmin, . . . , L} =
L20–L40 (21 layers, lmin = 0.5L) is the layer-selection and layer-wise-evaluation space used in
Section 4.1, Section 5.5 and Appendix C.2; it additionally contains L40 so that the fixed-last-layer
baseline of Table 10 falls inside it. The three depth stages of Appendix C.1 are the shallow-collapse
region L1–L17, the emergence band L18–L27, and the high plateau from L28 onward.
Metric conventions.
Throughout the paper and appendices, AUC denotes frame-level ROC-AUC
and AP denotes frame-level average precision. Unless stated otherwise, all scores are reported in
percent. Where a quantity is a raw score rather than a percentage (Figures 4b–c), this is stated in the
caption.

Permutation protocol and p-value convention.
To test the null hypothesis H0 that MoE routing
paths are independent of visual anomaly category, we run N = 2,000 Monte Carlo label permuta-
tions. All reported p-values use the standard add-one estimator ˆp = (b + 1)/(N + 1), where b is the
number of permutations whose statistic equals or exceeds the observed value. With N = 2,000 this
bounds the attainable resolution at ˆp = 0.0005, which is the value reported whenever b = 0. Two
tests are performed:
1. Omnibus test. Category labels are shuffled across all 23,582 anomalous clips to build
the permutation distribution of the mean off-diagonal JS divergence. The observed global
mean is ¯DJS = 0.04812 nat with b = 0, hence ˆp = 0.0005.
2. Pairwise tests. For each pair (yi, yj), clips are pooled and randomly partitioned into equal-
sized surrogate groups. As shown in Figure 5a, every pair attains b = 0 and therefore
ˆp = 0.0005, rejecting H0 at the resolution limit of the test.

describe the same outcome, and the former states the attainable bound explicitly.
Interpretation.
Visually distinct anomaly types invoke measurably different sparse computational
sub-networks in the frozen backbone. The largest separation is between the global texture eruptions
of Explosion and the localized multi-agent kinematics of Fighting (DJS = 0.084 nat). We note
that the tests establish that routing distributions differ across categories; they do not establish that
the activated expert sets are disjoint, and Figure 5b shows substantial directional overlap for several
pairs.

Architectural constraints for RoMF.
Two design constraints follow.
1. Multiplicative modulation over concatenation. The verified channel-level correspon-
dence indicates that routing signals are coupled to specific semantic coordinates.
A
channel-wise scaling vector γt generated from Rclip(t) mirrors the feed-forward writing
mechanism, whereas plain concatenation [ ¯Ht; Rclip(t)] discards the coordinate mapping
and permits unconstrained shortcut fitting. This is verified empirically in Appendix E.1.
2. Selection bounded to the middle-to-late range. Because channel–expert alignment is
absent in shallow layers, adaptive layer selection restricts its candidate space to L =
{lmin, . . . , L}. Modulating at shallow depths would rescale channels that have not yet
received specialized expert writes.

Observations.
Three properties of these curves matter for the method.
First, the onset of the plateau is anchored at the same depth, L28, on all three benchmarks, even
though the datasets differ in domain (real versus synthetic), in imbalance ratio (from 11.7:1 normal-
heavy in UCF-Crime to 0.8:1 abnormal-heavy in UBnormal) and in absolute separability by an order
of magnitude. The transition therefore looks like a property of the backbone’s depth-wise computa-
tion rather than of any dataset, which is what makes a fixed candidate range L = {lmin, . . . , L} with
lmin = 0.5L a safe design choice across these benchmarks. We have verified this on three datasets
and one backbone family and do not claim it holds for arbitrary MoE models.
Second, absolute and relative separability rank the datasets in opposite orders. UBnormal has by
far the lowest absolute peak (0.1378) yet the largest relative gain (9.9×); UCF-Crime has a high
shallow baseline (0.195) driven by static scene identity, which inflates the floor and suppresses the
ratio to 2.8×. Any criterion used to pick a layer must therefore be scale-free with respect to the
dataset: it can compare layers within a dataset but not thresholds across datasets. The normalized
gap G(l) of Section 4.1 satisfies this, being invariant to a global rescaling of the hidden space.
Third, the plateau is what removes the need for an oracle. Inside it the choice of layer is nearly free:
the spread of J(l) over the plateau is 0.023 on XD-Violence, 0.031 on UCF-Crime and 0.010 on
UBnormal, one to two orders of magnitude smaller than the shallow-to-plateau jump. A selection
procedure only needs to land inside the band, not to find its argmax. The narrowest band, UBnor-
mal’s four layers, sets the tolerance budget and is the reason the depth regularization λreg is kept
small (Appendix C.2).
Finally, these macro-level curves should be read together with the channel-level analysis of Ap-
pendix B.3. Channel–expert alignment departs from chance at L19–L20, whereas linear Fisher
separability only emerges at L26–L28 and plateaus from L28. The ordering is consistent with the
chain of Section 3.2: the specialized experts begin writing anomaly-informative content into specific
hidden channels several layers before that content becomes linearly separable in the terminal query
representation.

and selects inside the emergence zone (L26 and below), where AP degrades sharply (−1.56 pp at

normal plateau spans only four layers, capping λreg at 0.1 keeps selection inside the plateau on all
three benchmarks, while the constraint l ≥lmin = 0.5L prevents modulating unspecialized shallow
channels.
Summary.
Three conclusions carry over to the main text. First, G(l) estimated from 5% weakly
labelled videos lands inside the plateau on all three benchmarks, so RoMod needs neither frame-
level annotation nor a post-hoc layer search; its argmax does not track the argmax of J or of the
downstream metric, and it is not designed to. Second, the residual gap to oracle selection (≤0.14
points) measures the flatness of the full model over the plateau rather than the accuracy of the crite-
rion. Third, because semantic discriminability is stable throughout the plateau, depth regularization
prevents drift towards the language-prediction layers without compromising detection accuracy.

This appendix specifies the category-balanced multiple-instance learning (C-MIL) procedure that
turns the 5% weak-label budget into the clip-level pseudo-labels ˆyt used by Stage II. D5% holds 5%
of the weakly labelled training videos; within its anomalous portion, videos are drawn uniformly
across the annotated anomaly categories so that rare event types are represented in proportion to the
taxonomy rather than to their raw frequency. The positive-mining effort is spent entirely on those
anomalous bags. Normal videos cost nothing to process: a Y = 0 video is a pure-negative bag,
so every clip it contains receives ˆyt = 0 without entering the refinement loop. Because l⋆is itself
selected from the pseudo-labels produced here, C-MIL cannot read features at l⋆; it operates on the
fixed reference layer lref = L. Each clip feature h(lref)

C-MIL does not start from random weights. The frozen language head supplies a zero-shot verdict

risks over-fitting the scorer to its own errors. A final normal-anchor peak suppression and Gaussian

SUPPLEMENTARY EXPERIMENTS AND IN-DEPTH ABLATIONS
E.1
COMPARISON OF FUSION OPERATORS
Protocol.
All variants are evaluated as a single fusion block followed by a linear scoring head, with
the temporal network RoTN removed, so that the comparison isolates the operator rather than down-
stream temporal smoothing. Every variant shares the frozen backbone, the same Stage-I pseudo-
labels induced from D5%, the same adaptively selected layer (l⋆= 33 for UCF-Crime, l⋆= 28 for
XD-Violence and UBnormal, cf. Appendix C.2), the same bottleneck width d, and the same opti-
mizer schedule. Every variant passes Rclip(t) through the identical L·E →d →D bottleneck and
differs only in how the resulting D-dimensional vector is combined with ¯Ht. The concatenation
head therefore consumes 2D input dimensions against D for the modulation variants, a difference
of D = 2048 parameters, under 0.03% of the trainable total. The RoMF row of Table 13 reproduces
exactly the RoMF-only row (✓/ ×) of Table 2 in the main text.
The routing vector alone is already linearly separable.
Before comparing operators we quantify
how much of a decision surface the routing branch can support on its own. Fitting a linear discrimi-
nant on the ℓ1-normalized routing statistics Rclip(t), with no hidden state and no temporal context,
yields Table 12: 87.16% AUC on UCF-Crime, 88.14% AP on XD-Violence against a 24.24% pos-
itive base rate, and 74.31% AUC on UBnormal. On UCF-Crime and UBnormal this exceeds the
hidden-only baseline of Table 2 (82.90% and 72.40%). The routing dimensions are thus not a weak
auxiliary cue but a self-sufficient classifier input, which is precisely the condition under which an
unconstrained fusion operator can dispense with the semantic pathway.
Operator ranking.
Table 13 orders the four operators identically on all three benchmarks: mul-
tiplicative residual > FiLM > additive > concatenation. Relative to direct concatenation, RoMF
gains 3.66 points of AUC on UBnormal, 2.48 on UCF-Crime and 2.32 AP on XD-Violence; relative
to additive fusion the margins are 2.79, 1.86 and 1.81. FiLM, which augments the multiplicative
scale with an unconstrained shift β, recovers most of the gain but stays consistently below RoMF
(−0.74, −0.33, −0.58), the shift term reintroducing exactly the additive routing component that the
pure multiplicative form excludes. The ordering is monotone in how much freedom the operator
grants the routing branch to act outside the semantic channels.

Concatenation collapses onto the routing branch.
The decisive comparison is not between fu-
sion operators but between concatenation and its own routing-only reference. Concatenation scores
74.26 on UBnormal, 87.32 on UCF-Crime and 88.63 AP on XD-Violence, i.e. within 0.5 points
of the routing-only linear probe on all three benchmarks, and 0.05 points below it on UBnormal.
Adding a 2048-dimensional semantic branch to an equally wide projected routing branch therefore
buys essentially nothing: the fused model is, to within noise, the routing probe of Table 12. RoMF,
by contrast, exceeds both single-branch references by 2.64–3.61 points, which is the signature of
genuine complementarity rather than of one branch dominating.
The mechanism is visible in the gradient structure. Under [ ¯H; WR] the classifier weight splits as
w = [wH; wR] and the logit decomposes additively, w⊤

can produce a response on channel c. With W2 zero-initialized the module starts at the identity, and
routing can only reweight evidence, never manufacture it.
Consequences for out-of-distribution transfer.
A weight vector placed directly on the routing
coordinates fits the routing prior of the training domain, which Appendix B.2 shows to be category-
and scene-dependent: normal clips sit at or slightly below the global average activation baseline,
and each anomaly type displaces a different depth band, with pairwise JS divergences up to 0.084
nat. Such a decision surface is tied to the activation statistics of the source distribution and does
not survive a change of appearance domain. Consistent with this, the gap between RoMF and
concatenation widens as the domain gap grows: it is 2.32 AP on XD-Violence, whose distribution
the pseudo-labels are drawn from, and 3.66 AUC on the synthetic UBnormal, whose renderings
share almost no appearance statistics with real surveillance footage and whose hidden-space Fisher
separability is the weakest of the three (Jpeak = 0.1378, Appendix C.1). Where the semantic branch
is weakest, an unconstrained operator leans hardest on the routing shortcut and transfers worst.
The multiplicative residual keeps the semantic pathway load-bearing by construction, which is why
RoMod retains 79.17% AUC on UBnormal in the full model. The domain-gap evidence behind this
reading is reported in Appendix E.2.
E.2
ZERO-SHOT AND CROSS-DOMAIN GENERALIZATION
To establish the capability boundaries and cross-domain robustness of frozen multimodal MoE back-
bones without any downstream adaptation, we evaluate their training-free zero-shot detection perfor-
mance across three benchmarks. In this regime, anomaly scores are read directly from the model’s
native textual output logits under an identical anomaly-querying prompt, bypassing intermediate
probes, routing modulation, and temporal context modelling.
As summarized in Table 14, all evaluated MoE backbones show substantial zero-shot discriminabil-
ity on the real-world surveillance benchmarks: XD-Violence AP reaches 71.37% on Qwen3.6-
35B-A3B and 76.65% on Qwen3VL-30B-A3B against a 24.24% chance level, and UCF-Crime
AUC reaches 74.95% against 50.00%. The three architectures are also consistent with one another
(Gemma-4-26B-A4B reaches 71.11% AP on XD-Violence against Qwen3.6’s 71.37%), which is
what one would expect if the native anomaly sensitivity came from the shared MoE routing paradigm
rather than a specific parameterization.
The training-free readout degrades sharply across visual domains, however: on the synthetic UB-
normal benchmark, zero-shot AUC falls to 57%–59%, indicating that generic language priors do not
transfer to non-photorealistic textures and synthetic motion. This domain gap is what RoMF and
RoTN have to close; under the same 5% weak supervision they raise UBnormal AUC to 79.17%
(Table 1).

LIMITATIONS
We state four limitations of the present study explicitly.
The dense comparison is a cost reference, not a controlled one.
Table 4 places a dense 27B
backbone in the same pipeline, where it runs 2.24× slower at 10.0× the per-step FLOPs. Two
things it does not settle. Total capacity is 27B against 35B, so the pair is close to but not exactly
matched. And a dense backbone has no routing to read, so RoMF is inapplicable and its accuracy
column reflects a hidden-only readout; the accuracy gap therefore cannot be charged to sparsity
alone.
Backbone strength and routing modulation are not fully separated.
The full-data baselines
in Table 1 use I3D, C3D or CLIP features, whereas RoMod reads a frozen 35B-scale MoE. The
ablations of Table 2 and Appendix E.1 isolate the contribution of RoMF and RoTN given this back-
bone, and Table 4 shows the effect persists across three backbones, but a same-backbone baseline
trained on 100% of the weak labels would be needed to attribute the margin over prior work between
representation quality and routing modulation.
The routing-only probe is already strong in-domain.
Table 12 shows that a linear head on
Rclip alone reaches 88.14% AP on XD-Violence. The full framework adds 4.35 AP on that bench-
mark. The larger part of RoMod’s value is therefore not raw in-domain accuracy but the constraint
that keeps the semantic pathway load-bearing, which is what shows up under domain shift (Ap-
pendix E.1) and across layers (Section 5.5). We report the routing-only number rather than omitting
it because it bounds how much of the result a trivial readout already achieves.
Several diagnostics consume test-set labels.
J(l) (Appendix C.1), S(l)
c
and Φ(l)
c (Appendix B.3),
and the expert ranking z(l)
e
used to choose the knockout set (Appendix B.4) are all estimated with
ground-truth temporal boundaries. They characterize the frozen backbone and are never read by
the deployed system, whose only layer-selection signal is G(l) on D5%. Read as mechanistic evi-
dence they are valid; read as a selection procedure they are not available at deployment time, and
Appendix C.2 exists to show the deployable substitute suffices.

EXTENDED QUALITATIVE VISUALIZATIONS
To examine the behaviour of RoMod under diverse surveillance conditions, this section provides
extended qualitative comparisons against the tuning-free hidden-state baseline across normal footage
and three real-world anomaly categories (riot, fighting, explosion).
Figures 10 and 11 show continuous frame-level anomaly curves with representative sampled
keyframes for 12 video sequences from XD-Violence and UCF-Crime. Ground-truth anomalous
segments are shaded pink, the decision threshold (τdec = 0.5) is the dashed red line, RoMod is solid
blue, and the zero-shot hidden probe is dashed orange.
H.1
IN-DEPTH QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS
Three behavioural regularities recur across the evaluated scenarios.
1.
False-alarm suppression in complex normal footage.
In purely normal sequences
(v=HksDvkX6Bjk, v=5AlAh8J3WqE, v=tI7fktKY6OU, v=fkps18H3SXY) the zero-shot
hidden baseline oscillates strongly. Camera panning, rapid lighting shifts and dense non-anomalous
pedestrian movement trigger spurious peaks that breach τdec = 0.5 and frequently exceed 0.8. Ro-
Mod holds the predicted score at or below 0.1 across all four videos. This is consistent with the
account in Section 4.2: routing vectors in normal videos stay close to the global empirical baseline,
so the multiplicative residual (1 + γt) leaves background semantic channels unamplified.
2. Multi-interval boundaries in crowd riots.
Long crowd-chaos sequences (v=gsz P8t-KM4,
v=OFxPTJxA5pg, v=Dh-3BlhAOnE) are difficult because of the lulls between successive

covers slowly during normal interludes, whereas RoMod follows the multi-segment structure. Even
