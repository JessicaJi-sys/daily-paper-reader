# Black-Box Knowledge Transfer across Distinct Feature Sets 

## Oh-Ran Kwon Department of Statistics, The Ohio State University Daeyoung Ham Department of Statistics and Data Science, University of Texas at San Antonio 

Abstract 

Pre-trained black-box predictive functions encode knowledge distilled from massive datasets and extensive computation. However, when the available input features differ from those the black box expects, direct use is infeasible. We introduce a method for transferring predictive knowledge from the black box to a new, heterogeneous input space. Our approach decomposes the target regression function into a transferable component, which the black box can inform, and a non-transferable component, which captures information unique to the new space. We propose a two-step neural network procedure, estimating the transferable component from abundant unlabeled feature pairs that bridge the two input spaces and the non-transferable component from limited labels. We derive prediction risk bounds that improve on those of a non-transfer alternative when the non-transferable component is small or smooth, and the procedure adapts to either case. Under additional conditions, the worst-case risk of our estimator is of strictly smaller polynomial order than the minimax risk of estimation from the labeled data alone. We extend the framework to multiple black boxes, each on its own input space, and show that aggregation can reduce prediction error relative to the best single black box. Simulated and real data demonstrate the practical value of the method. 

Keywords: heterogeneous transfer learning; black-box prediction; unlabeled paired data; nonparametric regression; deep neural networks. 

# 1 Introduction 

Modern pre-trained prediction functions achieve remarkable performance. The data and model structure used to train them, however, are often proprietary. Such functions are therefore used as “black boxes”. One can obtain their predictions at given inputs, but nothing more. Nevertheless, a black box encodes valuable predictive knowledge distilled from massive datasets and extensive computation. 1arXiv:2608.12403v1 [stat.ML] 10 Aug 2026 To apply a black box, inputs must lie in its input space. In practice, however, the same underlying quantity is often measured by different instruments that differ in resolution or representation. Waymo’s driving system, for instance, maps sensor measurements to driving actions (Bansal et al., 2018). Its sensor suite is regularly upgraded (Jeyachandran, 2024), and measurements from the new sensors no longer match the inputs on which the system was trained. Similarly, a clinical risk score built on one electronic health record system is often unusable at a hospital whose records encode different variables in different formats (Huang et al., 2020). In both cases, the available target features X ∈ X differ from the source features Z ∈ Z (̸= X ) that the black box requires, and so direct use is infeasible. A direct approach is to learn a new prediction function from scratch, using labeled pairs (X, Y ) collected on the new features. However, it discards the knowledge already encoded in the black box. A more practical approach takes advantage of what is readily available in current data collection environments. Unlabeled paired features ( X, Z ) are often machine-recorded and require no human annotation, so they are cheap and abundant. In autonomous driving, old-and-new sensor pairs can be generated by simulating driving scenarios (Fang et al., 2024). In healthcare, electronic health records capture many variables across large patient populations (Ehrenstein et al., 2019). Such pairs can bridge the two input domains and open a way to repurpose the predictive knowledge in the black box. This raises a fundamental statistical question: how can predictive knowledge in a black box be transferred to a different input domain? Figure 1 summarizes the problem.

To the best of our knowledge, existing methods are not well suited to the two defining challenges of our setting: mismatched source and target feature spaces, and a black box whose training data and internals are inaccessible. Further, rigorous statistical guarantees are largely absent. We detail the related literature and these gaps in Section 1.1. One may regard imputation as the most natural attempt. It regresses Z on X over DA

and plugs the imputed features into the black box (possibly with a further correction step). 2This approach, however, requires the black box to be stable under perturbations of its inputs. Modern black boxes are highly nonlinear, so even small imputation errors can produce large deviations in the output. (We elaborate on this point and compare imputation with our method in Section 4.3.2.) This paper takes a different route. We transfer the black box’s prediction itself into the target feature space, approximating bf (Z) by a function of X. The starting point is an elementary decomposition of the target regression function:

Both steps use deep ReLU (rectified linear unit) networks, which approximate a broad class of functions (Schmidt-Hieber, 2020; Kohler and Langer, 2021). Our theory provides prediction risk bounds for the two-step estimator and discusses when it improves on non-transfer learning (Section 4). We find that transfer is beneficial in two regimes: (i) when the black box is accurate, so that ∥δ∥2 is small and little is left to estimate; and (ii) when the black box explains the complex part of g, so that δ is smooth and can be learned from few labels. Our estimator adaptively attains the favorable regime. Under certain conditions, the benefit of transfer is fundamental in the sense that the worst-case risk of our estimator is of strictly smaller polynomial order than the minimax risk of any estimator that uses the labeled data alone (Theorem 3). Finally, we extend the framework to multiple black boxes, each defined on its own feature space (Section 5). We apply the two-step procedure to each black box and combine the resulting predictors into an ensemble. We show that the ensemble converges at the rate of the best single black box, without requiring prior knowledge of which it is. Moreover, the ensemble can strictly improve on it when the black boxes are comparably accurate but distinct. 

## 1.1 Related literature 

Our problem can be viewed as transfer learning, which has been studied extensively in recent years. Transfer learning broadly aims to improve performance on a target domain by 3leveraging information from a source domain. Analogously, we leverage a black box defined on the source domain to construct a predictive function on the target space. Most attention has been devoted to homogeneous transfer learning, where the source and target spaces coincide. Their models are assumed to differ (i.e., posterior drift) only in small or structured ways. This setting has been studied for high-dimensional linear regression (Li et al., 2022), generalized linear models (Tian and Feng, 2023; Li et al., 2024), multi-task linear regression (Zhao et al., 2026a), nonparametric regression (Cai and Pu, 2024), nonparametric classification (Cai and Wei, 2021; Reeve et al., 2021; Auddy et al., 2025), quantile regression (Bai et al., 2024), graphical models (Li et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2026b), and contextual bandits (Cai et al., 2024). Several of these works additionally allow covariate shift, where the input space is the same but the distributions differ (He et al., 2024). Heterogeneous transfer learning, where the feature spaces differ, has received limited at-tention in statistics. It has been studied more extensively in machine learning, primarily through algorithmic developments. See Day and Khoshgoftaar (2017) and Bao et al. (2023) for comprehensive reviews. Existing approaches are broadly feature-based or model-based. Feature-based methods make the source and target features comparable, for example by map-ping both into a common space (Duan et al., 2012; Wang and Breckon, 2022), transforming one space into the other (Kulis et al., 2011), or bridging them through intermediate domains (Tan et al., 2015). Model-based methods transfer at the parameter level, either weakly sharing parameters between source and target models (Shu et al., 2015) or transforming those of a trained source model for the target (Ye et al., 2020). Both typically require more than a black box, whether the source training data or the source model’s internals, which proprietary constraints withhold. Our method requires neither. Rigorous statistical guarantees for heterogeneous transfer learning are also largely absent. An exception is Chang et al. (2024), where the target features form a subset of the source features and both regressions are linear. In contrast, we impose no structural relationship between the two feature spaces and allow both regressions to be highly nonlinear. Beyond transfer learning, our work connects to prediction-powered inference (Angelopou-los et al., 2023a) and follow-up work (Angelopoulos et al., 2023b; Motwani and Witten, 2023; Zrnic and Cand` es, 2024), which use black-box predictions on unlabeled data to sharpen inference for finite-dimensional parameters. Recent extensions estimate conditional mean functions in parametric settings (Shan et al., 2025) or in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (Sui et al., 2026). Our framework instead targets a broader nonparametric function class, and, more fundamentally, our black box operates on a different feature space, so it cannot 4be evaluated on the target data. Nevertheless, both approaches conceptually treat black-box outputs as proxies for the response and refine the target quantity using limited labeled data. Our work also relates to semi-supervised learning (Van Engelen and Hoos, 2020), which, like ours, uses abundant unlabeled data to estimate a target more efficiently than from the labels alone. Its statistical benefits have been studied for linear regression (Chakrabortty and Cai, 2018; Azriel et al., 2022), M-estimation (Song et al., 2024), and high-dimensional settings (Zhang and Bradic, 2022; Deng et al., 2024). The difference is in the unlabeled data and its use. In semi-supervised learning, it is target covariates within a single feature space, used to exploit its distribution. In ours, it is pairs of source and target features, used to bridge two feature spaces.

## 3.2 Two-step procedure with adaptive selection 

We describe each step of Algorithm 1. In the first step (line 2), we treat the black-box predictions bf (Zj ) as pseudo-responses and regress them on Xj over F(Lh, ph), using DA.This yields the estimator bh of the transferable component. In the second step (line 3), we estimate the non-transferable component from the labeled data. We split DT into a training set Dtr and a validation set Dval , and fit the residuals 

Yk − bh(Xk) on the training set with a network eδ ∈ F (Lδ, pδ). The role of the validation set is as follows. As discussed in Section 2.2, transfer is advantageous when δ has small magnitude or when δ is smooth, but which case holds is unknown in practice. If ∥δ∥2 is small, the residuals are mostly noise. Fitting them from the limited nT observations typically adds variance without reducing bias, and eδ is better discarded. If δ is smooth, eδ typically removes more bias than the variance it adds, even with small nT , and is worth keeping. This trade-off can be viewed as a choice of whether to use the estimate eδ. The validation set makes this choice among the candidates bgλ = bh + λ eδ,

λ ∈ { 0, 1}, and the selected bλ defines the final estimator bgbλ (line 4). In practice, the architectures ( Lh, ph) and ( Lδ, pδ) are tuning parameters, and they can be chosen by validation. Candidates for ( Lh, ph) are evaluated on a held-out portion of DA,and candidates for ( Lδ, pδ) are evaluated jointly with λ on Dval , since the two selections minimize the same least squares.

## 4.3 When transfer helps

In contrast, imputation-based learning can be competitive when Z is nearly determined by X and bf is regular enough that imputation errors do not propagate. Section S5 of the Supplementary Material gives the risk bound and its rate, under an L2-rate condition on bm, second-order conditions on bf , and a compositional condition on bf {m(·)} − h.

We extend Algorithm 1. The procedure again consists of two steps. # 8 Discussion 

We study transfer learning from a black box whose feature space differs from the target’s. Our two-step estimator decomposes the target regression g into h, the part the black box explains, plus δ, the part it leaves out, and estimates each from the sample suited to it. Our prediction risk bounds show that transfer succeeds either when the black box is accurate, so that δ is small, or when it captures the complex part of g, so that δ is simple to learn. Our two-step scheme can accommodate any estimator at each step. Because each step is a regression on the target features, methods such as kernels, random forests, or boosting apply, and the two steps need not use the same one. This can be useful in applications, where the preferred estimator may depend on the domain and the available data. Extending the theory to other component estimators would be an interesting direction. Our theory concerns prediction. Inference is a natural next step, such as confidence bands for g or for the non-transferable component δ, the latter aided by the Neyman-orthogonal second step of Remark 2. This would also connect our framework to prediction-powered inference, discussed in Section 1.1. 23 Acknowledgments
